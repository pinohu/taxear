Death does not close the books. Income the decedent had earned but not yet received cannot be taxed on
the final return, because the decedent never received it — and it cannot escape tax, because § 1014(c)
denies the step-up in basis that would otherwise wipe it out. Section 691 resolves that by taxing the
item to whoever actually receives it, on the same terms the decedent would have faced.

## The rule

**What it is, and who is taxed.** {fig:ird.definition} The three limbs run in sequence: the estate if
the right passed to it, the person who acquires the right by reason of the death if it did not, and the
legatee where the estate has already distributed the right (IRC § 691(a)(1)(A)–(C)). Only one applies to
any given item.

**No step-up** (IRC § 1014(c)). {fig:ird.no_basis_step_up} This is the provision that makes the whole
topic necessary.
Ordinary inherited property takes a date-of-death basis and the appreciation is never taxed; an item of
income in respect of a decedent takes the decedent's basis and the income is taxed in full.

**Character is inherited** (IRC § 691(a)(3)). {fig:ird.character} A right to a long-term capital gain
remains a long-term capital gain in the beneficiary's hands, with the decedent's holding period. A right to ordinary income
— accrued salary, a final commission, a deferred bonus, the taxable part of a traditional retirement
account — remains ordinary.

**Transferring the right accelerates the income** (IRC § 691(a)(2)). {fig:ird.transfer} The exception
matters as much as the rule: passing the right at death, or distributing it to the person entitled, is
not a transfer.
Selling it is, and so is satisfying an installment obligation at other than face value.

**Deductions in respect of a decedent** (IRC § 691(b)). {fig:ird.deductions} They are the mirror image
of the income side, and they follow the obligation rather than the property.

**The estate tax deduction** (IRC § 691(c)). {fig:ird.estate_tax_deduction} Without it the same value
would be taxed twice — once in the gross estate and once as income. The deduction is not the whole estate tax; it is
the estate tax attributable to the net value of the items, allocated by the ratio the particular item
bears to all of them.

**Annuities.** Section 691(d) treats amounts received by a surviving annuitant under a joint and
survivor annuity contract, where the decedent annuitant died after the annuity starting date, as
included under § 691(a) for the purposes of computing the estate tax deduction — during the surviving
annuitant's life expectancy period and to the extent included in gross income under § 72.

## How it works in practice

**Ask first whether the decedent had a right to the amount at death.** That is the test. A completed
sale with proceeds not yet received produces an item; a sale the executor negotiates after death does
not. Accrued but unpaid salary is an item; salary for work the decedent never performed is not.

**Then find the recipient.** Follow the right, not the money. Whoever had the right when the payment
arrived reports it, and the § 691(a)(1) sequence answers that question in every ordinary case.

**Then carry the character across.** The recipient reports it the way the decedent would have — same
character, same holding period, same ordinary or capital treatment. This is where the answer usually
lies on an examination question.

**Then look for the estate tax deduction.** If a federal estate tax was actually paid, the recipient has
a deduction for the portion attributable to their item. It is frequently missed, because it requires the
estate tax return and the beneficiary's income tax return to be looked at together, often by different
advisers in different years. And it survives the suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions, so it
is worth having.

The most common item on an ordinary return is not exotic: a traditional individual retirement account or
qualified plan inherited by a beneficiary is income in respect of a decedent to the extent of the
taxable portion. That is why an inherited retirement account is taxed on distribution while an inherited
brokerage account is not.

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The sale that settled after death</h3>

Adaeze held listed shares for ten years and sold them at a $3,000 gain. She died before the proceeds
reached her account, and the money was paid to her son as the beneficiary of the account.

The right to the proceeds is an item of income in respect of a decedent. Section 691(a)(3) treats it in
his hands as if acquired in the transaction in which the right to the income was originally derived, so
it is a long-term capital gain and carries her holding period — not ordinary income, and not a
short-term gain measured from the date of death. Section 1014(c) denies any step-up, so the whole $3,000
is taxed to him.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>Selling the right instead of collecting it</h3>

An estate holds a right to a $40,000 deferred consulting fee. Rather than wait, the executor assigns the
right to an unrelated buyer for $34,000 at a time when its fair market value is $36,000.

Section 691(a)(2) requires the estate to include the fair market value of the right at the time of
transfer — $36,000 — even though only $34,000 was received, because the consideration did not exceed
fair market value. Had the buyer paid $38,000, the estate would include $38,000: the fair market value
plus the excess. Simply distributing the right to a residuary beneficiary would have been no transfer at
all, and the beneficiary would report the fee when collected.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The deduction nobody claimed</h3>

A beneficiary receives $120,000 from an inherited traditional individual retirement account over two
years. The estate paid federal estate tax, and the account was one of several items of income in
respect of a decedent in the gross estate.

The $120,000 is ordinary income with no step-up. But § 691(c)(1)(A) gives the beneficiary a deduction
for the estate tax attributable to that item, computed as the ratio the value of the account bears to
the net value of all such items, applied to the estate tax attributable to that net value — and spread
across the years in which the income is reported. It is an itemized deduction, and § 67(b)(7) takes it
out of the definition of a miscellaneous itemized deduction, so the § 67(h) disallowance does not touch
it. The practical obstacle is informational: the beneficiary needs figures from the estate tax return.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**No step-up in basis.** Section 1014(c) is the whole reason the item is taxable. A beneficiary who
assumes an inherited asset comes with a fresh basis will under-report by the entire amount.

**Character carries over.** Capital stays capital and ordinary stays ordinary, with the decedent's
holding period. The date of death does not restart anything.

**Selling the right is a taxable event on the value, not the price.** Section 691(a)(2) uses fair market
value at the time of transfer, plus any excess consideration — so a sale at a discount does not reduce
the income.

**Distributing the right is not a transfer.** Neither is transmission at death. Only the estate's or
beneficiary's disposition of the right triggers § 691(a)(2).

**The estate tax deduction requires estate tax to have been paid.** With no federal estate tax there is
no deduction, however large the item.

**It survives the miscellaneous itemized deduction disallowance.** Section 67(b)(7) names § 691(c)
expressly, so a page describing all itemized deductions other than the named few as gone has this one
wrong.

**Deductions in respect of a decedent follow the obligation.** Where the estate is not liable, the
deduction goes to whoever took the property subject to it — not automatically to the estate.

**An inherited retirement account is the everyday case.** Most returns that involve this topic involve
nothing more exotic than that.
</div>

## How this has changed

Section 691 has not been amended in substance for many years, and nothing in Pub. L. 119-21 touched it,
§ 1014, or § 67(b)(7).

What has changed around it is the environment in which it operates, in two ways that matter to a
practitioner.

First, the estate tax deduction has become simultaneously more valuable and less often available. More
valuable because the suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions swept away most other itemized
deductions while § 67(b)(7) kept this one; less often available because the estate tax exemption is now
high enough that most estates pay no federal estate tax at all, and without estate tax there is nothing
to deduct. The result is a provision that is close to irrelevant for most estates and unusually
worthwhile for the few it reaches.

Second, the most common item of income in respect of a decedent is now the inherited retirement account,
and the rules governing how quickly it must be distributed have changed twice since 2019. Section 691
itself does not care about the distribution schedule — it taxes what is received when it is received —
but the interaction determines how the income bunches, and therefore how much of the § 691(c) deduction
is used in each year. The distribution rules are covered on 1.2.2.l.

Section 691(c)(2)(B)'s cross-reference to § 421(c)(2) for statutory stock options remains in the
section, and is a reminder that this provision predates most of the retirement regime it now mostly
serves.

## Exam focus

The character question is the one that recurs: an item of income in respect of a decedent keeps the
character it would have had in the decedent's hands. Expect a fact pattern with a completed sale and a
death before payment, where the wrong answers offer ordinary income or a short-term gain measured from
the date of death.

Know that there is no basis step-up, and be able to say why — § 1014(c) — because that is the reason the
income exists at all.

Know the three recipients in § 691(a)(1) and that only one applies to a given item. Know that selling
the right accelerates the income at fair market value. And know that the § 691(c) deduction exists, that
it is an itemized deduction, and that § 67(b)(7) protects it.

## Check yourself

**1.** A taxpayer sold shares held for ten years at a gain and died before the proceeds were received.
The beneficiary collects them. What is the character of the income?

*Answer: Long-term capital gain. IRC § 691(a)(3) treats the right in the recipient's hands as if
acquired in the transaction in which the right to the income was originally derived, so both the
character and the decedent's holding period carry across.*

**2.** Why is an inherited traditional individual retirement account taxable when an inherited
brokerage account is not?

*Answer: Because IRC § 1014(c) excludes property constituting a right to receive an item of income in
respect of a decedent from the date-of-death basis rule. The retirement account is such a right, so it
takes no step-up and the taxable portion is income under § 691(a) when received; the brokerage account
takes a § 1014(a) basis and the pre-death appreciation is never taxed.*

**3.** An estate sells a right to future income with a fair market value of $50,000 for $44,000. What
does it include?

*Answer: $50,000. IRC § 691(a)(2) includes the fair market value of the right at the time of transfer,
plus the amount by which any consideration exceeds that value. A sale below value does not reduce the
inclusion.*

**4.** Does the § 691(c) deduction survive the disallowance of miscellaneous itemized deductions?

*Answer: Yes. IRC § 67(b)(7) excludes the deduction under § 691(c) from the definition of a
miscellaneous itemized deduction, so the § 67(h) disallowance does not reach it. It remains an itemized
deduction, so it is available only to a taxpayer who itemizes.*

**5.** A decedent's final medical practice expenses are paid by the residuary beneficiary who took the
practice subject to them, the estate having no liability. Who deducts them?

*Answer: The beneficiary. IRC § 691(b)(1)(B) allows the deduction to the person who, by reason of the
death or by bequest, devise or inheritance, acquires from the decedent an interest in property subject
to the obligation, where the estate is not liable to discharge it. The deduction is allowed in the
taxable year when paid.*
