There is no single military tax regime. What exists is a set of provisions scattered across the Code,
each solving a different problem, and they do not share definitions or mechanics. The one that produces
the most surprise is § 112: a member who sets foot in a combat zone on the last day of a month has that
entire month's compensation excluded.

## The rule

**The combat zone exclusion.** {fig:military.combat_exclusion} The asymmetry between ranks is the point
of the section: an enlisted member excludes everything, while an officer's exclusion is capped by what
the most senior enlisted member could earn. A commissioned warrant officer is treated as enlisted, not
as an officer (IRC § 112(c)(1)).

**The month rule.** {fig:military.month_rule} It works in both directions — a single day of qualifying
service exempts the month, and a member who leaves the zone on the first of a month still has that month
excluded.

**What a combat zone is.** {fig:military.combat_zone_designation} This is not a factual test about
danger. Without an Executive order there is no combat zone, whatever the conditions.

**What is not compensation.** {fig:military.not_compensation}

**Qualified military benefits.** {fig:military.qualified_benefits} Section 134(b)(2) then freezes the
category: no benefit is a qualified military benefit unless it was excludable on that 1986 date, so the
exclusion cannot expand by administrative practice. A basic allowance for housing or subsistence is
excluded on this footing rather than by any provision naming it.

**Deadlines stop running.** {fig:military.deadline_suspension} Section 7508 reaches acts by the taxpayer
and acts by the Service alike — assessment, collection and levy are suspended along with filing and
payment. It also covers someone serving *in support of* the Armed Forces in the zone, and a deployment
outside the United States on a designated contingency operation, which does not require a combat zone at
all.

**Forgiveness on death.** {fig:military.death_forgiveness} The relief reaches back to the first year in
which the member served in the zone, and § 692(a)(2) abates tax already assessed for earlier years.
Section 692 extends the same treatment to deaths from terroristic or military action and to certain
specified attacks.

**Moving expenses.** {fig:military.moving_expenses}

**The principal residence exclusion.** {fig:military.residence_suspension} Without the election, a
member posted abroad for four years could fail the two-out-of-five-years use test on a home they still
own.

**Reservist travel.** {fig:military.reservist_travel} This is an above-the-line deduction, so it
survives the suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions entirely.

**Combat pay and the earned income credit.** A member may elect to treat § 112 excluded pay as earned
income for the earned income credit (IRC § 32(c)(2)(B)(vi)). The election is optional and can move the
credit in either direction — see 1.3.2.e.

## How it works in practice

**Start with the pay stub, not the return.** Combat zone pay is normally already excluded from the
wages reported in box 1 of the Form W-2, with the excluded amount shown separately in box 12 under code
Q. The return usually does not need to do anything about it — but the amount matters for the earned
income credit election, for individual retirement account contributions, and for the self-employment
tax base if the member also has a business.

**Then check the calendar rather than the days.** The question is which months contain a day of
qualifying service, not how many days were served.

**Then check rank.** An enlisted member or warrant officer excludes all of it; a commissioned officer is
capped, and the cap is a monthly figure that changes with the military pay tables.

**Then look for the collateral provisions.** A deployment usually triggers more than one: deadlines
suspended under § 7508, a possible § 121(d)(9) election on a home left behind, a permanent change of
station move under § 217(g), and reservist travel if the member serves in a reserve component.

**And remember what the exclusion costs.** Excluded pay is not compensation for retirement plan
purposes, is outside earned income unless elected in for the earned income credit, and does not build
the earnings record. A long deployment can reduce a member's individual retirement account contribution
room to nothing without anyone noticing.

<div class="scenario">
<h3>One day, one month</h3>

Sergeant Bello's unit arrives in a designated combat zone on 29 March and returns on 2 October. Her
monthly basic pay is $4,300.

Every month from March through October contains at least one day of qualifying service, so all eight
months of compensation are excluded under § 112(a) — including March, of which only three days were
served, and October, of which two were. Nothing is prorated. Had her return been delayed to 1 November,
a ninth month would have been excluded in full.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The officer's ceiling</h3>

Captain Marek serves in the same zone for the whole of a month. His basic pay for the month is $8,900,
and he receives imminent danger pay of $225.

Section 112(b) excludes only so much as does not exceed the maximum enlisted amount, which
§ 112(c)(5) defines as the highest basic pay for an enlisted member at the top pay grade for that month
plus, for an officer receiving hostile fire or imminent danger pay, the amount of that special pay. So
his exclusion is that ceiling and the excess is taxable. A warrant officer in the same billet, by
contrast, would exclude the lot: § 112(c)(1) says a commissioned warrant officer is not a commissioned
officer for this purpose.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The house they could not sell</h3>

Petty Officer Nakamura and her spouse bought a home in 2019, lived in it until a 2021 posting overseas
on qualified official extended duty, and sold it in 2026 without having lived in it again.

Without relief the two-out-of-five-years test in § 121(a) fails. Section 121(d)(9)(A) lets them elect to
suspend the running of the five-year period during the qualified official extended duty, capped at ten
years by subparagraph (B). With the suspension the look-back reaches into the period before the posting,
the use test is met, and the gain is excluded up to the ordinary limit. The election is made for the
property, and it is worth asking about on every military client who owns a home.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**A day is a month.** Service in a combat zone during any part of a month excludes that whole month's
compensation. There is no proration and no minimum period.

**Officers are capped, enlisted members and warrant officers are not.** And a commissioned warrant
officer counts as a warrant officer.

**Pensions and retirement pay never qualify** (IRC § 112(c)(4)), whenever or wherever the service was
performed.

**No Executive order, no combat zone.** Danger is not the test.

**Section 7508 suspends the Service's clock too.** Assessment, collection and levy stop along with
filing and payment, and it applies to a contingency operation outside any combat zone.

**Section 134 is frozen at 9 September 1986.** A benefit not excludable under an outside law, regulation
or practice on that date cannot become a qualified military benefit now.

**The moving expense deduction survived only for the military** — and, from 2026, for the intelligence
community. Everyone else lost it permanently.

**Excluded pay is not earned income by default.** The earned income credit election under
§ 32(c)(2)(B)(vi) is optional and has to be considered both ways.

**The § 121(d)(9) suspension is an election**, not automatic, and it is capped at ten years.
</div>

## How this has changed

The military provisions were untouched by Pub. L. 119-21 with one exception, and the exception is worth
knowing precisely because it is easy to state too broadly.

Section 70113(a) of that Act amended § 217(k) — the suspension of the moving expense deduction — by
substituting "beginning after 2017" for "2018 through 2025" in the heading and striking the 1 January
2026 end date from the text. The suspension is now permanent. It always carried an exception for a
member of the Armed Forces within § 217(g), and that exception is unaffected: a military move under
orders incident to a permanent change of station still qualifies, still ignores the § 217(c) distance
and time tests, and still allows in-kind moves and allowances to be excluded from income.

Section 70113(b) then added § 217(k)(2), which treats an employee or new appointee of the intelligence
community — other than a member of the Armed Forces — who moves pursuant to a change in assignment
requiring relocation in the same manner as a person to whom § 217(g) applies. Both amendments apply to
taxable years beginning after 31 December 2025. So the deduction now has two surviving classes rather
than one.

Nothing else on this page moved. Sections 112, 134, 692 and 7508 carry no 2025 amendments, and § 121(d)(9)
is unchanged. The figure that does move every year is the maximum enlisted amount, because it tracks the
military pay tables rather than an inflation adjustment — it is a monthly figure, and it should be taken
from the pay tables for the month concerned rather than from any annualised summary.

## Exam focus

The month rule is the most likely question and it is usually posed as a proration trap: a member serves
part of a month, and the wrong answers divide the pay by days. The right answer is the whole month.

Know the enlisted-versus-officer asymmetry and that a warrant officer follows the enlisted rule. Know
that pensions and retirement pay are outside § 112 entirely.

Know that § 7508 gives the service period plus 180 days and suspends the Service's deadlines as well as
the taxpayer's, and that it reaches contingency operations outside any combat zone.

Know that the moving expense deduction survives for the military, and for 2026 also for the intelligence
community, while it is permanently gone for everyone else. And know the § 121(d)(9) election, because it
is the provision most often missed on a real return.

## Check yourself

**1.** An enlisted member serves in a designated combat zone from 27 June to 4 July. How much
compensation is excluded?

*Answer: All of June's and all of July's. IRC § 112(a) excludes compensation for active service for any
month "during any part of which" the member served in a combat zone, so both months are excluded in
full with no proration for the days actually served.*

**2.** Does a commissioned officer exclude combat zone pay without limit?

*Answer: No. IRC § 112(b) limits the exclusion to the maximum enlisted amount — the highest basic pay
for an enlisted member at the highest pay grade for that month, plus hostile fire or imminent danger pay
where the officer receives it. A commissioned warrant officer is outside this limit by § 112(c)(1).*

**3.** A member is hospitalised in the United States for wounds received in a combat zone. Is the pay
still excluded?

*Answer: Yes, for months of continuous hospitalisation attributable to the wounds, but not for a month
beginning more than two years after combatant activities in that zone terminate (IRC § 112(a)(2),
(b)(2)). The same hospitalisation period also extends the § 7508 suspension of deadlines.*

**4.** May a civilian employee who moves for a new job deduct the cost?

*Answer: No. IRC § 217(k)(1) suspends the deduction for taxable years beginning after 2017 and Pub. L.
119-21 § 70113(a) made that permanent. Two classes survive: a member of the Armed Forces moving under
orders on a permanent change of station (§ 217(g)), and from 2026 an employee or new appointee of the
intelligence community relocating on assignment (§ 217(k)(2)).*

**5.** A service member deployed for six years sells the home they lived in before deploying. Can the
§ 121 exclusion still apply?

*Answer: Yes, if they elect under IRC § 121(d)(9)(A) to suspend the running of the five-year period
during the qualified official extended duty. The suspension is capped at ten years by § 121(d)(9)(B),
and it is an election rather than an automatic rule.*
