<div class="plain-terms">
A tax return is rarely a clean, stand-alone event. What happens one year can change what a later return
may claim. A loss from an earlier year can quietly cut what is owed now. This topic covers seven such
carryovers: things like a business loss, an investment loss, or an unused credit that a taxpayer can
carry into a future year, or in some cases back to a past one. It affects almost anyone with a loss, a
large credit, or a business, not just people with unusual finances. It decides how much of an old loss or
credit is still good, for how long, and under what cap, so nothing gets lost just because nobody wrote it
down.
</div>

An individual return can carry seven different things forward, and no two of them behave alike. The
periods differ, the annual limits differ, some keep their character and some do not, and one of them —
the negative qualified business income carryover — leaves no trace on the return that generated it. The
single most expensive routine error in individual practice is a carryover that gets lost when a client
changes preparer.

## The rule

**Net operating losses.** {fig:carryover.nol_period} And {fig:carryover.nol_limit}. Two vintages
therefore coexist: pre-2018 losses expire after twenty years but are deductible in full, and post-2017
losses never expire but are capped. Where a taxpayer holds both, the older vintage is absorbed
first (IRC § 172(a)(2)(A)).

**Capital losses.** {fig:carryover.capital_loss} The character rule (IRC § 1212(b)(1)) is what most
questions turn on: the excess of net short-term loss over net long-term gain carries as a short-term
loss, and the excess of net long-term loss over net short-term gain carries as a long-term loss. It is
not converted, and it is not merged.

**Charitable contributions.** {fig:carryover.charitable} The carryover is used after the current year's
contributions, and it is subject to the same percentage limitation in the year it is used.

**Passive losses.** {fig:carryover.passive} The carryover is activity-by-activity, which is why a client
disposing of one rental does not automatically free up suspended losses from another.

**Negative qualified business income.** {fig:carryover.negative_qbi} This one is easy to lose because
the year it arises produces no deduction and often no visible entry — the loss simply reduces next
year's deduction, and a preparer who has not seen the prior year's computation will not know it exists.

**The minimum tax credit.** {fig:carryover.minimum_tax_credit} Covered at 1.4.1.a.

**The general business credit.** An unused general business credit is carried back one year and forward
twenty (IRC § 39(a)). It is worth knowing what it is *not*: the minimum tax credit is a separate provision
(IRC § 53) and is not a component of the general business credit (IRC § 38(b)), whatever the form numbers suggest.

## How it works in practice

**Build a carryover schedule and carry it forward with the file.** It should list, for each carryover,
the amount, the year it arose, the expiry if any, and the character. This is the single most useful
working paper on an individual engagement and the one most often absent when a file arrives from another
firm.

**Ask for the prior two returns, not just the last one.** A carryover that was suspended in one year and
unused in the next is invisible on the most recent return.

**Apply the character rule to capital losses before netting.** Long-term carryover offsets long-term
gain first; short-term carryover offsets short-term gain first; only the excess crosses over.

**Watch the vintage on net operating losses.** A client with losses from both sides of 2018 has two
pools with different rules, and taking them in the wrong order wastes the older one.

**And ask about qualified business income even in a loss year.** The negative amount has to be recorded
somewhere, because nothing on the return will remind anyone of it next year.

<div class="scenario" data-type="baseline">
<h3>The carryover that changed character</h3>

In the prior year a taxpayer realised an $8,500 long-term {gloss:capital-loss} and a $3,000 short-term
{gloss:capital-gain}. She
deducted the maximum against ordinary income. This year she has a $1,500 long-term gain and a $3,000
short-term gain.

Prior year: the $3,000 short-term gain nets against the long-term loss, leaving a $5,500 net long-term
loss. Three thousand of it is deducted, and $2,500 carries forward as a *long-term* loss under
§ 1212(b)(1)(B). This year: the $2,500 long-term carryover offsets the $1,500 long-term gain first,
leaving $1,000 of long-term loss, which then reduces the $3,000 short-term gain. The result is a $2,000
net short-term gain — taxed at ordinary rates, not at the long-term rate the client may be expecting.
</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="interaction">
<h3>Two pools of losses</h3>

A consultant has a $60,000 {gloss:net-operating-loss} carryover from 2016 and a $200,000 net operating
loss carryover from 2022. This year her taxable income before any net operating loss deduction is $150,000.

The 2016 loss is deductible in full and is taken first under § 172(a)(2)(A) — $60,000, leaving $90,000.
The 2022 loss is then limited by § 172(a)(2)(B)(ii) to 80 percent of the $150,000 figure, or $120,000,
but only $90,000 of income remains, so $90,000 is used. She has no taxable income, $110,000 of the 2022
loss carries forward indefinitely, and the 2016 pool is exhausted. Taking the 2022 loss first would have
left the 2016 loss to expire unused.
</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="timing">
<h3>The loss nobody recorded</h3>

A sole proprietor has a $40,000 loss from her only business this year and no other qualified business
income. Her return shows no qualified business income deduction, which is unsurprising, and nothing else
about § 199A.

Next year the business makes $70,000. A preparer working only from this year's return computes a
deduction on $70,000. The correct figure is $30,000, because § 199A(c)(2) treats the $40,000 as a loss
from a qualified trade or business in the succeeding year. The error is worth $8,000 of deduction and it
is invisible unless someone wrote the negative amount down.
</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="boundary">
<h3>The separate return that halves the limit</h3>

A married taxpayer filing separately has a $9,000 net capital loss for the year and no capital gains. Her
spouse, filing his own separate return, has no capital transactions at all this year.

She can deduct only $1,500 of the loss against ordinary income this year, not the $3,000 allowed on a
joint or single return — the annual limit under § 1211(b) is exactly halved for a separate return. The
remaining $7,500 carries forward, still subject to the same halved limit each year the couple continues to
file separately. Filing jointly instead would double what she can absorb in a single year, at the cost of
making both spouses liable for the return as a whole.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**Post-2017 net operating losses never expire but are capped at 80 percent.** Pre-2018 losses expire
after twenty years and are not capped. Both rules can apply to one client.

**Capital loss carryovers keep their character.** Long-term stays long-term. It does not become
short-term with the passage of time.

**The annual capital loss allowance is halved on a separate return.**

**Charitable carryovers last five years and are used after current contributions**, subject again to the
percentage limitation.

**Passive losses are tracked per activity**, and are released on a fully taxable disposition of the
entire interest in *that* activity.

**A negative qualified business income amount carries forward** even though the year it arose shows no
deduction.

**The minimum tax credit comes only from deferral items.** Exclusion items generate nothing.

**Form 8801 is not a general business credit component.** The minimum tax credit under § 53 is separate
from the § 38 general business credit.
</div>

## How this has changed

The net operating loss rules are the ones that changed most, and they changed twice in quick succession.

Pub. L. 115-97 removed the general two-year carryback and the twenty-year expiry for losses arising in
taxable years beginning after 31 December 2017, and introduced the 80 percent limitation. Pub. L. 116-136
then temporarily reversed both for losses arising in taxable years beginning after 31 December 2017 and
before 1 January 2021 — § 172(b)(1)(D) gave those a five-year carryback and suspended the limitation.
That window has closed, but the provision remains in the section and a reader who does not check its
dates will apply a carryback that no longer exists.

The result is three vintages of net operating loss with three sets of rules, and § 172 as printed
contains all of them at once. This is the same reading problem recorded at 1.3.2.g for the premium tax
credit and at 1.4.1.l for the shared responsibility payment: **the operative rule depends on a date
limit buried inside the provision, not on the provision's headline.**

Nothing in Pub. L. 119-21 amended § 172, § 1212, § 170(d), § 469 or § 53. Section 199A was made
permanent — covered at 1.3.1.h — which incidentally makes the § 199A(c)(2) carryover a permanent feature
rather than one that would have lapsed after 2025.

## Exam focus

Expect the capital loss carryover question, and expect it to turn on character. Net the prior year
correctly, carry the excess with its character intact, and apply it against the same character first.

Know that post-2017 net operating losses are indefinite and capped at 80 percent, that pre-2018 losses
expire after twenty years and are not capped, and that the older pool is used first.

Know the five-year charitable carryover and that passive losses are suspended per activity until a fully
taxable disposition.

Know that a negative qualified business income amount carries to the next year, and that the minimum tax
credit is separate from the general business credit.

## Check yourself

**1.** A taxpayer carries forward a $2,500 long-term capital loss into a year with a $1,500 long-term
gain and a $3,000 short-term gain. What is the result?

*Answer: A $2,000 net short-term gain. Under IRC § 1212(b)(1)(B) the carryover retains its long-term
character, offsets the long-term gain first, and the remaining $1,000 then reduces the short-term gain.*

**2.** Which is not a component of the general business credit — the investment credit, the minimum tax
credit, the work opportunity credit or the energy efficient home credit?

*Answer: The minimum tax credit. It is allowed under IRC § 53 as a credit for prior year minimum tax
liability and is not among the credits aggregated into the general business credit under § 38.*

**3.** How long may an individual carry forward a charitable contribution that exceeded the percentage
limitation?

*Answer: Five succeeding taxable years, in order of time, under IRC § 170(d)(1)(A). In each of those
years it is used after the current year's contributions and remains subject to the applicable percentage
limitation.*

**4.** A client has net operating losses from 2016 and 2022. Which is used first?

*Answer: The 2016 loss. IRC § 172(a)(2)(A) allows losses arising in taxable years beginning before
1 January 2018 in full and before the post-2017 pool, which is separately limited to 80 percent of
taxable income computed without the § 172, § 199A and § 250 deductions.*

**5.** A business has a qualified business income loss this year and profits next year. What happens?

*Answer: IRC § 199A(c)(2) treats the negative net amount as a loss from a qualified trade or business in
the succeeding taxable year, reducing next year's qualified business income and therefore the deduction.
Nothing on the loss year's return records it, so it has to be tracked deliberately.*
