Most of the value in retirement advice sits in four decisions, and none of them is about which fund to
buy. When distributions have to start. Who is named as beneficiary. Whether charitable giving should
come out of the account rather than out of the current account. And whether the contribution is
deductible at all, which turns on a phase-out most clients do not know exists.

## The rule

**When distributions must start.** {fig:retplan.rmd_age} Two points are frequently got wrong. The
"later of" formulation means a still-working employee can defer past the applicable age — but not a
5-percent owner, and never for an individual retirement account, where the applicable age alone controls
(IRC § 401(a)(9)(C)(ii)).

**The penalty for missing one.** Twenty-five percent of the shortfall, reduced where corrected promptly
— covered at 1.4.1.l and 1.2.2.h. The figure of 50 percent that appears in older material is no longer
the law.

**Who inherits, and what they can do.** {fig:retplan.spousal_rollover} This single distinction produces
more planning value than any other on the topic: a spouse beneficiary can defer for a further lifetime,
while a non-spouse cannot and is instead subject to the distribution rules covered at 1.2.2.l.

**Giving from the account.** A qualified charitable distribution is {fig:retplan.qcd_conditions}. The
2026 limits are {fig:retplan.qcd_limit}. The amount is excluded from gross income rather than deducted,
which is why it is worth more than writing a cheque: it never enters adjusted gross income, so it does
not push up the taxable portion of social security, does not raise Medicare premium brackets, and does
not require itemising.

**And the trap inside it.** {fig:retplan.qcd_offset} A client who keeps making deductible IRA
contributions after 70½ while also making qualified charitable distributions is quietly cancelling one
against the other.

**Whether the contribution is deductible.** {fig:retplan.ira_deduction_phaseout} The phase-out applies
only to an *active participant* in an employer plan — a client with no workplace plan deducts in full at
any income. And {fig:retplan.catch_up}

**Getting at the money early.** Section 72(t) imposes an additional 10 percent on the includible portion
of an early distribution, subject to the exceptions in § 72(t)(2) — covered at 1.2.2.h. The planning
point is that the exception list differs between plans and IRAs, so the same client fact can produce
different answers depending on where the money sits.

## How it works in practice

**Start with the beneficiary designation, not the account.** It overrides the will, it is frequently
stale, and it determines whether the account can be stretched or must be emptied. Reviewing it is the
cheapest valuable thing a preparer does for a client with a retirement account.

**Then fix the required beginning date on the calendar.** For an IRA it is driven by age alone. For an
employer plan the client may be able to defer while still working — unless they own 5 percent or more.
A client with several accounts may have different dates for different accounts.

**Then ask whether the client gives to charity at all.** If they do and they are over 70½, the
distribution route is almost always better than a cheque, and it is better even for a client who
itemises. The exclusion keeps the amount out of adjusted gross income entirely.

**Then check active participant status before promising a deduction.** The Form W-2 retirement plan box
answers it. A client whose spouse is covered but who is not has a much higher threshold of their own.

**And be careful with a client who is both contributing and giving.** The § 408(d)(8)(A) offset reduces
the excludable charitable amount by cumulative post-70½ deductible contributions, and it is cumulative
across years — it does not reset.

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The nephew who cannot roll over</h3>

An unmarried taxpayer dies at 45 leaving a $45,000 traditional IRA to a nephew.

The nephew may take the balance as a lump sum, over a period of years under the applicable rules, or as
an annuity where the account permits. What he cannot do is roll it into an IRA of his own. Only a
surviving spouse may treat an inherited account as their own or roll it over; every other beneficiary
takes it as an inherited account and must distribute it under § 401(a)(9)(B). A rollover attempted by a
non-spouse is a taxable distribution of the whole balance.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The cheque that should have been a distribution</h3>

Miriam is 74, takes a required distribution of $40,000, and separately writes $15,000 of cheques to her
church and a food bank. She does not itemise.

She has $40,000 of income and no charitable deduction, because the standard deduction is larger than her
itemised total. Had the $15,000 gone directly from the IRA trustee to the charities as a qualified
charitable distribution, it would have been excluded from gross income and would also have counted
toward her required distribution. Her adjusted gross income would have been $15,000 lower, which would
in turn have reduced the taxable portion of her social security. The gift was the same; the routing cost
her several thousand dollars.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The contribution that ate the exclusion</h3>

Olu is 76, still consults part-time, and has deducted a $7,000 IRA contribution in each of the last four
years. This year he makes a $20,000 qualified charitable distribution.

Section 408(d)(8)(A) reduces the excludable amount by the aggregate deductible contributions made for
years ending on or after he attained 70½, less reductions already applied. Four years at $7,000 is
$28,000, so his $20,000 distribution is excluded to the extent of nothing — it is fully includible, and
$8,000 of offset remains to reduce future distributions. He gets a charitable deduction only if he
itemises. The two strategies are individually sound and mutually destructive.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**A non-spouse beneficiary cannot roll over.** The option belongs to a surviving spouse alone.

**The still-working exception never applies to an IRA**, and never to a 5-percent owner of the employer.

**A qualified charitable distribution is an exclusion, not a deduction.** That is the whole of its
advantage, and it survives the standard deduction.

**It requires age 70½, not the required beginning date.** The two ages have diverged and the earlier one
governs here.

**Post-70½ deductible contributions reduce the exclusion cumulatively.** The offset does not reset each
year.

**It must go directly from trustee to charity.** A distribution to the client who then writes a cheque is
not a qualified charitable distribution.

**Donor advised funds and supporting organizations do not qualify** (IRC § 408(d)(8)(B)(i)).

**The deduction phase-out needs active participant status.** No workplace plan means no phase-out at any
income level.

**The excise tax for a missed distribution is 25 percent, not 50.**
</div>

## How this has changed

Nothing in Pub. L. 119-21 amended the provisions on this page. What has moved is the accumulation of
SECURE and SECURE 2.0 changes now fully in force, and the annual figures.

The applicable age is the change most likely to be misstated. Section 401(a)(9)(C)(v) now contains two
ages rather than one: 73 for an individual attaining 72 after 2022 and 73 before 2033, and 75 for one
attaining 74 after 2032. A source giving 70½ predates 2019; one giving 72 predates 2023; one giving a
single age of 73 without the later step is incomplete. Note also that 70½ has not disappeared — it
survives as the qualified charitable distribution age in § 408(d)(8)(B)(ii), so a client can be old
enough to give from the account years before they are required to take anything out of it.

The qualified charitable distribution limits are now indexed and both moved for 2026:
{fig:retplan.qcd_limit} Both were fixed amounts for many years, and material quoting the older figure is
using the statutory base rather than the current one — {fig:retplan.qcd_statutory_base}. This is the
same pattern recorded elsewhere on this project: **the section alone gives the wrong number.**

The § 219(g) phase-out ranges also moved for 2026, and the married-filing-separately applicable amount
remains zero and is expressly not indexed.

## Exam focus

Know the applicable age in both of its versions and the "later of" rule with its two exceptions —
5-percent owners and IRAs.

Know that only a surviving spouse can roll over or treat an inherited account as their own. This is the
single most reliably tested point on the topic.

Know the qualified charitable distribution conditions: direct trustee transfer, age 70½, a § 170(b)(1)(A)
organization, not a donor advised fund or supporting organization, and only to the extent otherwise
includible. Know that it is an exclusion and that it counts toward the required distribution.

Know that the IRA deduction phase-out applies only to an active participant, and that a spouse's coverage
brings a separate and much higher range.

## Check yourself

**1.** A nephew inherits a traditional IRA. Which option is unavailable to him?

*Answer: Rolling the balance into an IRA of his own. Only a surviving spouse may treat an inherited
account as their own or roll it over; a non-spouse beneficiary must take it as an inherited account
under the IRC § 401(a)(9)(B) distribution rules. A lump sum, distributions over a period, or an annuity
where available are all possible.*

**2.** A 74-year-old still working for an unrelated employer owns 2 percent of it. When must
distributions from the employer plan begin?

*Answer: Not until 1 April of the year following retirement. IRC § 401(a)(9)(C)(i) uses the later of the
applicable age and retirement, and the § 401(a)(9)(C)(ii) exception for 5-percent owners does not reach a
2 percent holding. Any IRA of theirs is a different matter — the retirement limb never applies to one.*

**3.** Why is a qualified charitable distribution better than writing a cheque for a client who does not
itemise?

*Answer: Because it is an exclusion from gross income under IRC § 408(d)(8)(A) rather than a deduction.
The amount never enters adjusted gross income, so the benefit is available regardless of the standard
deduction, and the lower adjusted gross income can reduce the taxable portion of social security and
other income-linked amounts. It also counts toward the required distribution.*

**4.** A client aged 72 has deducted IRA contributions since turning 70½. Does that affect a qualified
charitable distribution?

*Answer: Yes. IRC § 408(d)(8)(A) reduces the excludable amount by the aggregate deductions allowed under
§ 219 for all taxable years ending on or after the individual attained age 70½, less reductions already
applied in earlier years. The offset is cumulative and does not reset.*

**5.** A single taxpayer with no workplace retirement plan and adjusted gross income of $200,000 wants to
deduct an IRA contribution. Can they?

*Answer: Yes, in full. The IRC § 219(g) phase-out applies only to an active participant in an employer
plan. With no such plan and no spouse who is a participant, there is no income limit on the deduction.*
