Section 529 was reshaped more thoroughly in 2025 than at any point since it was written, and the changes
did not all start on the same day. One took effect on the date of enactment, one for taxable years
beginning after 2025, and one is a permanent extension of a rule that was days from expiring. Advice
given from a 2024 understanding of this section will be wrong in three separate ways.

## The rule

**What a section 529 account is for.** Distributions are tax-free to the extent of qualified higher
education expenses — tuition, fees, books, supplies and equipment required for enrolment, and, within
limits, room and board for a student enrolled at least half time (IRC § 529(c)(3)(B), (e)(3)(A), (B)).

**Elementary and secondary schooling.** {fig:edplan.529_k12_limit} And the expenses that count have been
widened substantially: {fig:edplan.529_k12_expenses}

**Credentials, not just degrees.** {fig:edplan.529_credentialing} This opens the account to a client
whose child is training for a licensed trade rather than attending a university, and to continuing
education a working adult must take to keep a credential.

**Rolling into an ABLE account.** {fig:edplan.529_able_rollover}

**Coverdell accounts.** {fig:edplan.coverdell_limit} The contribution limit has not moved and is not
indexed, which is why these accounts have largely been superseded by § 529 plans — though a Coverdell
allows a wider range of elementary and secondary expenses than § 529 historically did, an advantage the
2025 changes have largely erased.

**Employer help.** {fig:edplan.employer_assistance} The student loan limb was a temporary provision
renewed twice and was due to lapse at the end of 2025; it is now permanent, which changes the advice to
an employer client considering whether to set up a programme.

**Student loan interest.** {fig:edplan.student_loan_interest} It is an above-the-line deduction, so it
survives the disallowance of miscellaneous itemized deductions and is available without itemising.

**The credits sit alongside.** The American Opportunity and Lifetime Learning credits are covered at
1.3.2.c. The coordination rule matters here: the same expenses cannot support both a tax-free § 529
distribution and an education credit, so a family with both should allocate deliberately rather than by
accident.

## How it works in practice

**Check the date before applying any of the new rules.** The widened list of school expenses applies to
distributions made after 4 July 2025. The doubled dollar limit applies to taxable years beginning after
31 December 2025. A distribution made in, say, September 2025 draws on the wider expense list but the
older limit.

**Then allocate expenses between the benefits.** Tuition paid from a § 529 distribution cannot also
generate an American Opportunity credit. Where a family has both a funded account and credit
eligibility, paying part of the tuition from other funds to preserve the credit is usually worth more
than the tax-free growth on the same dollars.

**Then consider who owns the account.** Ownership affects control and, historically, federal financial
aid treatment. The tax rules do not turn on it, but the advice does, and a grandparent-owned account is
a different planning object from a parent-owned one.

**Then remember the account is not the only lever.** An employer programme under § 127 now permanently
covers student loan payments, and the § 221 deduction is available above the line. For a client already
carrying loans, those two are often worth more than a new savings account.

<div class="scenario">
<h3>Two dates in one year</h3>

The Adekunle family paid $9,000 of private secondary school tuition in August 2025 and another $16,000
in March 2026, both from their son's § 529 account. They also bought $1,400 of curriculum materials in
each period.

For the 2025 payments, the wider expense list already applied — § 70413(a) took effect for distributions
made after 4 July 2025 — so the curriculum materials qualified. But the annual limit for that year was
still the older figure, so only part of the $9,000 was covered. For 2026 both changes apply: the limit
is $20,000 and the materials qualify, so the whole $17,400 is within it. One family, one account, two
different answers separated by a change of taxable year.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The credential that now qualifies</h3>

Rukayat's daughter leaves school to train as a licensed electrician through a recognised apprenticeship
credential programme, and later pays testing fees to obtain the licence and annual continuing education
fees to keep it.

All three are qualified postsecondary credentialing expenses under § 529(f)(1)(A) to (C), so the § 529
account funds them tax-free. Before the 2025 change none of it qualified unless the programme happened
to be run by an eligible educational institution, and a family in this position was left with a funded
account and no qualifying use for it. Note the continuing education limb in particular: it reaches
expenses incurred long after the training ends.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>Double-dipping by accident</h3>

The Marchettis pay $12,000 of university tuition entirely from a § 529 distribution and then claim the
American Opportunity credit on the same tuition.

The same expenses cannot do both jobs. Had they paid $4,000 of the tuition from a current account and
the remaining $8,000 from the plan, they would have preserved the full credit — worth $2,500 — while
still using the account for most of the cost. The account is the more flexible instrument and should be
used for the expenses the credit cannot reach, not for the first dollars.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**Two different effective dates in 2025.** Wider expenses from 4 July 2025; doubled limit from taxable
years beginning after 31 December 2025.

**The elementary and secondary limit is per beneficiary per year**, not per account and not per parent.

**Credentialing expenses are new.** A source saying § 529 covers only degree-granting institutions is
pre-2025.

**Coverdell contributions stop at age 18** and are capped at a figure that has never been indexed.

**Employer student loan assistance is permanent now** — it had been renewed twice as a temporary measure
and was due to lapse at the end of 2025.

**The § 127 exclusion is indexed for the first time**, from 2027.

**The student loan interest deduction is above the line**, so itemising is irrelevant to it.

**Expenses cannot serve two benefits.** A tax-free distribution and an education credit cannot rest on
the same dollars.
</div>

## How this has changed

Four amendments, three effective dates, and one of them retroactive to mid-year.

**Section 70413(a)** rewrote § 529(c)(7). The old text extended "qualified higher education expense" to
elementary and secondary *tuition* and nothing else. The new text lists eight categories, from curriculum
materials to educational therapies for students with disabilities. It applies to distributions made
after 4 July 2025 — the date of enactment — so it reached part of the 2025 tax year.

**Section 70413(b)** doubled the annual elementary and secondary limit, for taxable years beginning
after 31 December 2025. The Code's own amendment note records a drafting artefact worth knowing: the Act
directed that "the last sentence of section 529(e)(3)" be amended, and the codifier executed the change
in the concluding provisions of § 529(e)(3)(A) instead, "to reflect the probable intent of Congress".
This is the third instance of directory language not matching the Code as enacted that this project has
recorded.

**Section 70414** added § 529(e)(3)(C) and a new § 529(f) defining qualified postsecondary credentialing
expenses, extending the account to recognised credential programmes, the testing required to obtain a
credential and the continuing education required to keep one.

**Section 70117(a)** struck the 1 January 2026 end date from § 529(c)(3)(C)(i)(III), making the rollover
to an ABLE account permanent for taxable years beginning after 31 December 2025.

Separately, **§ 70412** made the § 127 exclusion for employer payments of student loan principal and
interest permanent by striking its own end date, and added § 127(d) indexing the annual figure from 2027
— the first indexation since that amount was set.

## Exam focus

Know that the elementary and secondary limit doubled and that the list of qualifying expenses at that
level is now much wider than tuition. Both are new and both are likely to be tested against the old
answer.

Know that § 529 now reaches recognised postsecondary credential programmes, including testing and
continuing education fees.

Know the Coverdell limits — the contribution cap, the cash requirement and the age-18 cut-off — and that
they have not moved.

Know that the § 127 exclusion covers student loan payments permanently and is now indexed, and that the
§ 221 deduction is above the line with a phase-out that moves annually.

And know the coordination rule: the same expenses cannot support both a tax-free distribution and an
education credit.

## Check yourself

**1.** What is the maximum § 529 distribution for elementary and secondary expenses in 2026?

*Answer: $20,000 per beneficiary for the year. Pub. L. 119-21 § 70413(b) doubled the previous $10,000
figure for taxable years beginning after 31 December 2025. Any source giving $10,000 is describing 2025
and earlier.*

**2.** May a § 529 account pay for private tutoring?

*Answer: Yes, within limits. IRC § 529(c)(7)(E) covers tuition for tutoring or educational classes
outside the home where the tutor is not related to the student and is either licensed as a teacher in
any State, has taught at an eligible educational institution, or is a subject matter expert in the
relevant subject.*

**3.** A client's son is in a recognised electrician credential programme. Can the § 529 account pay the
testing fee for the licence?

*Answer: Yes. IRC § 529(f)(1)(B) treats fees for testing required to obtain or maintain a recognised
postsecondary credential as qualified postsecondary credentialing expenses, which § 529(e)(3)(C) brings
within qualified higher education expenses.*

**4.** Can an employer permanently exclude payments toward an employee's student loans?

*Answer: Yes. Pub. L. 119-21 § 70412(a) struck the 1 January 2026 cut-off from IRC § 127(c)(1)(B), so
employer payments of principal or interest on a qualified education loan remain within the exclusion
indefinitely, subject to the annual limit — which § 127(d) now indexes from 2027.*

**5.** A family pays $12,000 of tuition entirely from a § 529 distribution and claims an education
credit on it. What is wrong?

*Answer: The same expenses cannot support both a tax-free qualified tuition program distribution and an
education credit. Allocating part of the tuition to other funds preserves the credit while still using
the account for the balance.*
