Signing a joint return makes each spouse liable for the whole tax, whoever earned the income and whoever
made the error (IRC § 6013(d)(3)). Section 6015 is the way out, and it is not one remedy but three, each
with its own eligibility test, its own deadline, and — critically — its own answer to whether money
already paid can come back.

## The rule

**The three routes.** {fig:innocent.three_routes}

**Subsection (b): the classic innocent spouse.** {fig:innocent.b_conditions} Where the requesting spouse
knew of some of the understatement but not its extent, § 6015(b)(2) apportions relief: they are relieved
of the part they did not know about. The knowledge test is the battleground — "did not know, and had no
reason to know" is judged on what a reasonable person in the same circumstances would have concluded
from the return and the household's finances.

**Subsection (c): allocation, for spouses now apart.** {fig:innocent.c_conditions} If it applies, the
requesting spouse's liability for the deficiency is limited to the portion allocable to them under
§ 6015(d) — broadly, the deficiency is split as though the couple had filed separately. The requesting
spouse bears the burden of proving the allocation (IRC § 6015(c)(2)), and the election is invalid as to
any item of which the Secretary shows they had actual knowledge when signing (IRC § 6015(c)(3)(C)).

**Subsection (f): equitable relief.** {fig:innocent.three_routes} It applies only where (b) and (c) do
not, and it is the only route that reaches an *unpaid* liability correctly reported on the return —
subsections (b) and (c) both require an understatement or a deficiency. A couple who filed accurately
and simply could not pay have no route but (f).

**The deadlines differ.** {fig:innocent.timing} This is the most consequential difference between the
three routes: the two-year clock on (b) and (c) runs from the start of collection activity, while (f)
is tied to the collection period or the refund claim period instead.

**The Tax Court.** {fig:innocent.tax_court} The six-month rule means a requesting spouse is not left
waiting indefinitely for a determination.

**Refunds.** {fig:innocent.refunds} The § 6015(c) bar catches people out: a taxpayer who has already
paid and elects allocation gets a liability reduction with nothing to show for it. Where money is to
come back, the request must be framed under (b) or (f).

**Res judicata.** Where a court decision for the same year has become final, it is conclusive except as
to a claim for relief that was not an issue in that proceeding (IRC § 6015(g)(2)).

## How it works in practice

**Ask first what kind of liability it is.** A deficiency or understatement can go down any of the three
routes. An amount correctly reported and unpaid can only go to § 6015(f). This single question disposes
of a great many enquiries.

**Then ask about the marriage.** Section 6015(c) is closed to a couple still married and living
together. Section 6015(b) and (f) are not.

**Then ask what the client knew.** Knowledge defeats (b) outright, defeats (c) as to the items actually
known, and is one factor among many under (f). The client's own account of what they knew is where these
cases are usually won or lost, and it should be taken carefully and early.

**Then check the clock.** Two years from the start of collection activity for (b) and (c). For (f), the
collection period for an unpaid amount, or the refund claim period for a paid one.

**Then decide what outcome is wanted.** If the goal is to recover money already paid, § 6015(c) is the
wrong door however well the client fits it.

One point of client management: relief under any route requires the Service to notify the other spouse
and give them an opportunity to participate. A client who has not told their former spouse that they are
seeking relief should be told that they will find out.

<div class="scenario">
<h3>Understatement the spouse could not have seen</h3>

Ifeoma and Bright filed jointly for 2023. Bright omitted $70,000 of consulting receipts. The Service
assessed a deficiency in 2026 and began collection against Ifeoma. She had no involvement in his
business, saw no unusual spending, and the return she signed showed a plausible income for the
household.

She elects under § 6015(b) within two years of the start of collection. The understatement is
attributable to his erroneous items, she establishes no knowledge and no reason to know, and on the
facts it would be inequitable to hold her liable. She is relieved of the tax, interest and penalties
attributable to the omission. Had she known about part of the omitted receipts but not the scale,
§ 6015(b)(2) would have relieved her only of the portion she did not know about.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The right relief, the wrong subsection</h3>

Marta divorced in 2024. A 2022 deficiency of $18,000 was assessed, all of it attributable to her
ex-husband's unreported income, and Marta paid $11,000 of it from her own funds before seeking advice.

She is squarely within § 6015(c): she is no longer married, and the deficiency allocates almost entirely
to him. But § 6015(g)(3) allows no credit or refund as a result of a § 6015(c) election, so the $11,000
already paid stays paid and only the remaining $7,000 is affected. Framing the request under § 6015(b)
or (f) instead — both of which permit a refund, subject to § 6511 — is what recovers her money. The
choice of subsection, not the strength of her facts, decides whether she is $11,000 better off.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>Reported accurately, never paid</h3>

Chidi and Ada filed a joint 2021 return showing $24,000 due and paid nothing. Ada left the marriage in
2022 with no assets, and the Service is now collecting from her.

There is no understatement and no deficiency, so § 6015(b) and § 6015(c) are both unavailable on their
own terms. Her only route is equitable relief under § 6015(f), which reaches an unpaid liability
provided the request is made before the § 6502 collection period expires. Whether she gets it turns on
all the facts and circumstances — the Service's published factors include marital status, economic
hardship, knowledge, legal obligation under a divorce decree, significant benefit and compliance since.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**Three routes, not one.** They have different eligibility, different deadlines and different outcomes,
and choosing wrongly can cost the client the refund.

**Only § 6015(f) reaches an unpaid but correctly reported liability.** Subsections (b) and (c) need an
understatement or a deficiency.

**A § 6015(c) election never produces a refund** (IRC § 6015(g)(3)). It limits liability going forward.

**Section 6015(c) needs the marriage to be over or the household separate** for the twelve months ending
on the election date.

**The two-year clock runs from collection activity**, not from the assessment, the return, or the
divorce.

**Actual knowledge is fatal to (c) as to the item known**, and the Secretary bears the burden of showing
it — one of the few places the burden sits there.

**A fraudulent transfer of assets between the spouses invalidates a (c) election entirely** (IRC
§ 6015(c)(3)(A)(ii)).

**The other spouse will be notified** and may participate. There is no confidential route.

**Six months, then the Tax Court.** A requesting spouse need not wait for a determination beyond six
months to petition.
</div>

## How this has changed

Section 6015 was enacted in its present three-route form by the Internal Revenue Service Restructuring
and Reform Act of 1998, replacing a much narrower predecessor, and Pub. L. 119-21 did not amend it.

Two changes since are worth carrying. The § 6015(f) limitation period in current law is the one in
§ 6015(f)(2): a request as to an unpaid amount may be made at any time before the § 6502 collection
period expires, and as to a paid amount within the period for a timely refund claim. That replaced an
administrative two-year rule the Service had applied to equitable relief by regulation, which litigation
and then legislation displaced. Any source stating a flat two-year deadline for *all* § 6015 relief is
describing the position before that change, and it is the single most common error on this topic.

The other is the Tax Court's jurisdiction. Section 6015(e) now gives a stand-alone right to petition,
including for equitable relief under (f), and the six-month trigger in § 6015(e)(1)(A)(i)(II) means the
Service's delay does not bar the courthouse. A source describing § 6015(f) as reviewable only for abuse
of discretion, or as outside the Tax Court's reach, predates the current text.

## Exam focus

Know the three routes and be able to place a fact pattern in the right one. The two discriminators are
the type of liability — understatement or deficiency versus correctly reported and unpaid — and the
marital status.

Know the deadlines, and in particular that the two-year rule attaches to (b) and (c) and not to (f).

Know that § 6015(c) produces no refund, because that is the trap most likely to be tested and the one
that costs a real client real money.

Know the contrast with an injured spouse claim, covered at 1.5.1.h: relief from liability versus
allocation of a refund.

## Check yourself

**1.** A couple filed accurately, owe $30,000 they never paid, and have since separated. Which route is
available?

*Answer: Equitable relief under IRC § 6015(f) only. Subsections (b) and (c) require an understatement or
a deficiency, and there is neither — the liability was correctly reported. The request must be made
before the § 6502 collection period expires.*

**2.** A divorced taxpayer elects allocation under § 6015(c) after paying part of the deficiency. What
happens to the payment?

*Answer: Nothing. IRC § 6015(g)(3) allows no credit or refund as a result of a § 6015(c) election. To
recover an amount already paid the request has to rest on § 6015(b) or § 6015(f), subject to the § 6511
refund claim period.*

**3.** When does the two-year period for a § 6015(b) election begin?

*Answer: On the date the Secretary began collection activities with respect to the individual making the
election (IRC § 6015(b)(1)(E)) — not on assessment, not on filing, and not on divorce.*

**4.** A requesting spouse knew of part of an understatement but not its size. What relief is available
under § 6015(b)?

*Answer: Partial. IRC § 6015(b)(2) relieves the individual of liability to the extent attributable to
the portion of the understatement of which they did not know and had no reason to know, provided the
other conditions are met.*

**5.** Six months after filing a request, the Service has not determined it. What can the taxpayer do?

*Answer: Petition the Tax Court. IRC § 6015(e)(1)(A)(i)(II) allows a petition at any time after the date
six months after the election or request, without waiting for a final determination notice; where such a
notice is issued, the petition must be filed within 90 days of it.*
