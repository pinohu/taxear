<div class="plain-terms">
Every tax return has a line above the signature that says, under penalty of perjury, that it is true and
correct. Almost nobody reads it, but it matters a great deal. Signing a return you know has a lie in it is
a crime, even if it does not change the tax you owe. This topic affects anyone who signs a tax return, an
amended return, or many of the other forms and statements filed with the IRS. The crime does not need the
IRS to lose any money to exist. It decides what a taxpayer is really promising by signing, and what can go
wrong, in court and in dollars, if that promise turns out to be false.
</div>

Every return a client signs carries a declaration above the signature line, and almost every client
signs it without reading it. It is worth explaining once, properly, because it is the provision that
turns a return problem into a criminal one. The declaration is what makes IRC § 7206(1) available, and
§ 7206(1) is a felony that does not require the government to prove any tax was owed at all.

## The rule

**The declaration is mandatory.** {fig:perjury.declaration_required} It is not a feature of Form 1040 —
it is a statutory requirement reaching every return, declaration, statement and other document under the
internal revenue laws.

**So is the signature.** {fig:perjury.signature_required} And once it is there,
{fig:perjury.signature_presumption} A client who says the signature is not his bears the burden of
displacing that presumption.

**What the declaration exposes the signer to.** {fig:perjury.7206_elements} Three things about that are
worth saying out loud. The offence is complete on signing and filing; no deficiency need result; and
"material" does not mean "large" — a false statement about anything the return calls for can be
material.

**The sentence is not the one printed.** {fig:perjury.7206_sentence}

**Two neighbouring offences.** {fig:perjury.7207_offence} And the one everyone has heard of:
{fig:perjury.7201_sentence} Evasion requires an affirmative act and an unpaid tax; § 7206(1) requires
neither.

**And the civil counterpart.** {fig:perjury.civil_fraud} The burden allocation (IRC § 6663(b)) is the
part that matters in practice — once any portion is established as fraudulent, the taxpayer has to
disaggregate the rest.

## How it works in practice

**The declaration reaches more than the Form 1040.** The requirement applies to any document required
under the internal revenue laws or regulations (IRC § 6065), it covers amended returns, most elections, Forms 433
submitted in collection, protests, and the great majority of statements attached to a return. A client
who is casual about an attached statement is being casual under the same declaration as the return
itself.

**"Believes to be true and correct as to every material matter" is the standard.** Not "is correct" —
the offence turns on the signer's belief. That cuts both ways. A client who signs a return he knows
omits income has committed the offence even if the omission turns out to be immaterial to the tax; a
client who signs a return that is wrong, but which he honestly believed to be right, has not.

**Willfulness is what the government has to prove, and it is defined narrowly.** The standard is the
voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty. Misunderstanding, disorganisation and reliance
on a preparer are inconsistent with it. This is why the advice to a client with a problem return is
almost always to correct it: a voluntary correction is difficult to reconcile with an intention to
violate a known duty.

**The preparer's position is different but not distant.** A preparer does not sign under penalties of
perjury in the same declaration — the preparer's declaration is its own — but the aiding provision (IRC § 7206(2)) reaches
willfully aiding or assisting in the preparation of a return that is false as to a material matter,
regardless of whether the taxpayer knew. Part 3 covers the preparer side; the point for an individual
adviser is that a client's insistence does not insulate anyone.

**Signing a joint return signs it for both.** The declaration is made by each spouse who signs, and each
is separately exposed. This is worth pairing with the joint and several liability discussion at 1.5.1.m
when a client asks whether to sign a return prepared from the other spouse's records.

**What to do with a client who will not correct.** Decline the engagement rather than sign the return.
There is no version of this in which continuing is the safe option.

<div class="scenario" data-type="baseline">
<h3>The return with no tax due and a felony in it</h3>

A client reports his consulting income accurately but claims $9,000 of business expenses he knows he did
not incur. His other deductions are large enough that his tax is zero either way, and the disallowance
would change nothing.

He has still committed the § 7206(1) offence. The elements are willfully making and subscribing a
document verified by a penalties-of-perjury declaration that he does not believe to be true and correct
as to every material matter. A deduction he knows to be fabricated is a material matter, and the section
nowhere requires an underpayment. That the tax is unchanged is a sentencing consideration, not a
defence.

</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="boundary">
<h3>The statement attached to the return</h3>

A client attaches a statement supporting a reasonable cause position, in which she overstates the
duration of an illness to make the excuse read better. The return itself is accurate.

The statement is within § 6065 — it is a document required under the internal revenue laws and
regulations and carries the declaration. The exposure is the same as if the falsehood had been on the
face of the return. Clients treat attachments as narrative rather than as sworn statements, and it is
worth correcting that impression before they draft one.

</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="interaction">
<h3>The signature the client did not make</h3>

A separated client learns that a joint return was filed for a year in which he and his wife had not
spoken. His name is on it. He says he never signed.

IRC § 6064 makes the signature prima facie evidence that he signed, so the starting position is against
him and he carries the work of displacing it. Whether the return is a valid joint return at all is a
separate question turning on his intent to file jointly; and if it is treated as joint, the joint and
several liability at 1.5.1.m attaches, with relief under § 6015 the only route out. Three different
provisions, one signature.

</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="procedural">
<h3>The fine that is not in the section</h3>

A client convicted under § 7206(1) reads the section, sees a maximum fine of $100,000, and asks how the
court arrived at $180,000.

The section's figure has been superseded. Under 18 U.S.C. § 3571(b)(3) an individual convicted of a
felony may be fined up to $250,000, and § 3571(e) preserves a lower statutory figure only where the
offence provision exempts itself by specific reference — which § 7206 does not do. The number printed in
the Code is not the number that applies, and the same is true of § 7201.

</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="fails">
<h3>Wrong, but not willful</h3>

A client took a {gloss:home-office-deduction} based on her preparer's advice that a guest room used a few
evenings a week for client calls qualified. It did not, and the deduction is disallowed on audit.

There is no § 7206(1) offense here. The government must prove she was {gloss:willful} — that she
voluntarily and intentionally violated a known legal duty she was aware of — and reliance on a preparer's
advice about a genuinely debatable position cuts against that. The declaration exposes a client who signs
a return she does not believe is accurate; it does not turn an honest, if wrong, position into a felony.
The disallowance is a civil adjustment. There is no {gloss:civil-fraud-penalty} here, and no criminal
referral.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**Thinking a false return without tax due is harmless.** IRC § 7206(1) has no deficiency element. It is
the single most common misunderstanding on this topic.

**Reading the fine out of the Code section.** 18 U.S.C. § 3571 supersedes the printed amounts for both
§ 7206 and § 7201. The imprisonment terms in the sections — three years and five years — are current;
the dollar figures are not.

**Confusing § 7206(1) with evasion.** Evasion under § 7201 needs an affirmative act of evasion and a tax
owing. Section 7206(1) needs a signed false document. Neither is a lesser included version of the other.

**Assuming the declaration covers only the return.** Section 6065 reaches any document required under
the internal revenue laws or regulations, including elections, attached statements and collection
information statements.

**Treating civil fraud and criminal fraud as the same finding.** IRC § 6663 is a 75 percent addition to
tax proved by the Secretary to a civil standard; the criminal provisions require proof beyond reasonable
doubt. A civil fraud penalty is not a conviction, and no conviction is needed for it.

**Overlooking § 6663(b).** Once the Secretary establishes that *any* portion of an underpayment is
attributable to fraud, the entire underpayment is treated as fraudulent unless the taxpayer establishes
otherwise. The 75 percent applies far more widely than clients expect.

**Letting a client sign "just to get it filed" while a question is open.** An unresolved question is a
reason not to sign yet, not a reason to sign and amend.

</div>

## How this has changed

Almost nothing here has moved. Sections 6061, 6064, 6065, 7201, 7206 and 7207 all date from the 1954
Code and carry their original operative text; § 6663 took its current form in the Improved Penalty
Administration and Compliance Tax Act of 1989, which separated civil fraud from the accuracy-related
penalty. Pub. L. 119-21 amended none of them.

The one thing that has changed, and changed permanently, is the relationship between the printed fines
and the applicable ones. The Criminal Fine Enforcement Act of 1984 and the Sentencing Reform Act put the
governing maxima into 18 U.S.C. § 3571, and the Code sections were never conformed. Every criminal tax
provision in title 26 that prints a dollar figure now prints a superseded one, and there is nothing in
the sections themselves to say so. This is the clearest example on the site of a rule that cannot be
stated correctly from its own section.

The other development is practical: § 6061(b) now requires the Secretary to maintain procedures for
electronic signatures, and the self-select PIN a client enters is the signature and carries the same
declaration. Clients who would hesitate over a pen do not hesitate over a PIN, which is a reason to
explain the declaration at the point the return is authorised rather than assuming a signature line
communicates it.

## Exam focus

The distinctions are what get tested. Know that § 7206(1) is a felony requiring no tax deficiency, that
§ 7207 is a misdemeanor with a one-year maximum, and that § 7201 evasion is a felony with a five-year
maximum requiring an affirmative act.

Know that willfulness means the voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty, and that
§ 6065 reaches documents beyond the return itself.

On the civil side, know the 75 percent rate and, more importantly, the burden rule: the Secretary
establishes one portion and the whole underpayment is treated as fraudulent until the taxpayer
disaggregates it.

## Check yourself

**1.** A taxpayer signs a return containing a deduction she knows is fabricated. The correction would
produce no additional tax. Has she committed an offence under IRC § 7206(1)?

*Answer: Yes. Section 7206(1) requires willfully making and subscribing a document verified by a
penalties-of-perjury declaration that the signer does not believe to be true and correct as to every
material matter. There is no requirement that any tax be due.*

**2.** What is the maximum term of imprisonment under IRC § 7206, and under IRC § 7201?

*Answer: 3 years under § 7206 and 5 years under § 7201. Both are felonies; the imprisonment terms
printed in those sections are current, unlike the fines.*

**3.** A taxpayer denies signing a return that bears his name. What is the evidentiary starting point?

*Answer: The signature is prima facie evidence for all purposes that he signed it, under IRC § 6064. The
burden of displacing that presumption is his.*

**4.** The Secretary establishes that $12,000 of a $40,000 underpayment is attributable to fraud. To
what amount does the 75 percent penalty apply?

*Answer: To the whole $40,000, unless the taxpayer establishes that a portion is not attributable to
fraud. IRC § 6663(b) treats the entire underpayment as attributable to fraud once any portion is
established as such.*

**5.** Does the penalties-of-perjury declaration apply to a statement attached to a return?

*Answer: Yes. IRC § 6065 applies to any return, declaration, statement or other document required to be
made under any provision of the internal revenue laws or regulations, except as the Secretary otherwise
provides.*
