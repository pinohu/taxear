For most clients the question has stopped being how to avoid estate tax. The exclusion is now large
enough, and permanent enough, that a family with a comfortable but ordinary estate will owe none — and
the planning that used to reduce the taxable estate now often costs the family more in income tax than
it saves. The central contrast on this page is between § 1014 and § 1015, not between taxable and exempt.

## The rule

**The exclusion.** {fig:estate.basic_exclusion} It is the amount behind the credit (IRC § 2010(c)(1)),
and the same figure supports the gift tax credit under § 2505(a)(1). The system is unified: lifetime
gifts consume the exclusion available at death.

**Generation-skipping transfers.** {fig:estate.gst_exemption}

**The annual exclusion.** {fig:estate.annual_exclusion} (IRC § 2503(b)). It is per donee, per year, and
unlimited in the number of donees — the reason systematic annual gifting remains the simplest transfer technique. Gifts
of future interests do not qualify, which is why a trust needs withdrawal rights to make its gifts
present interests.

**Gifts to a non-citizen spouse.** {fig:estate.noncitizen_spouse} The unlimited marital deduction is not
available where the donee spouse is not a United States citizen, and this larger annual figure replaces
it.

**Portability.** {fig:estate.portability} The election is the trap: it requires a return the estate would
otherwise have had no reason to file, and missing it forfeits an exclusion worth millions.

**Basis is where the money now is** (IRC §§ 1014(a), 1015(a)). {fig:estate.basis_contrast} That
contrast drives most current planning. Property held until death gets a fresh basis and the appreciation escapes income tax
permanently; the same property given away in life carries its old basis to the donee, who pays tax on
the whole gain when they sell. For an estate below the exclusion, giving appreciated property away is
usually the worse answer.

**And not everything gets a fresh basis.** Section 1014(c) excludes a right to receive an item of income
in respect of a decedent — covered at 1.4.1.h — so a retirement account passes with the decedent's basis
and full taxability.

**Life insurance** (IRC §§ 101(a)(1), 2042). {fig:estate.life_insurance} The two provisions do
different jobs and are constantly
confused: the proceeds are free of *income* tax under § 101(a)(1) whoever owns the policy, but they are
in the *gross estate* under § 2042 if the decedent had any incident of ownership. Moving ownership to an
irrevocable trust is what takes the proceeds out of the estate, and it does nothing about income tax
because there was never any income tax to avoid.

**Charitable giving.** A transfer to a qualifying charity is deductible for estate tax under § 2055 and
for gift tax under § 2522, and a lifetime gift is deductible for income tax under § 170 as well — the
one technique that reaches both systems.

## How it works in practice

**Size the estate against the exclusion first.** For a married couple with a portability election
available, the effective figure is twice the single exclusion. A great many estates that once needed
planning now do not.

**Then ask the basis question for every appreciated asset.** Holding until death converts unrealised
appreciation into a permanent income tax saving. Giving it away transfers the gain to the donee. For an
estate comfortably below the exclusion, the answer is almost always to hold.

**Then use the annual exclusion for what it is good at** — moving cash and low-basis-irrelevant assets
out of the estate without touching the lifetime exclusion, to as many donees as the client likes.

**Then check the insurance.** Ask who owns the policy, not who receives the proceeds. Ownership is what
§ 2042 turns on.

**And do not skip the portability return.** An estate below the filing threshold still has to file to
make the election, and the deadline is unforgiving in a way the underlying tax is not.

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The gift that cost more than it saved</h3>

Adaora, a widow with an estate of about $4 million, gives her daughter shares worth $300,000 that she
bought for $40,000, to "reduce the estate".

Her estate was never going to be taxable — it is far below the exclusion — so the gift saves no estate
tax at all. Under § 1015(a) her daughter takes Adaora's $40,000 basis, so a later sale produces a
$260,000 gain. Had the shares passed at death, § 1014(a) would have given a basis equal to fair market
value and the entire appreciation would have escaped income tax. The gift converted a permanent income
tax saving into a taxable gain, and cost part of the annual exclusion to do it.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The policy nobody moved</h3>

Bram dies owning a $2 million term life policy on his own life, payable to his children. His other
assets are modest.

The children receive the $2 million free of income tax under § 101(a)(1). But § 2042(2) includes the
proceeds in his gross estate because he possessed incidents of ownership at death — he owned the policy.
On these facts the estate is still below the exclusion so nothing is payable, but on a larger estate the
$2 million would have been taxable, and transferring ownership to an irrevocable trust well before death
would have kept it out. Note what the trust does not do: the proceeds were never subject to income tax
in the first place.
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The return that had to be filed</h3>

Chidinma dies in 2026 with an estate of $3 million, all passing to her husband. Her executor sees no
estate tax and no filing requirement, and files nothing.

Her unused exclusion — the full amount, since the marital deduction covered the entire estate — is lost.
Section 2010(c)(5)(A) makes the deceased spousal unused exclusion amount available only where the first
estate filed a timely and complete estate tax return electing portability. Her husband's own exclusion is
unaffected, but the couple has forfeited a second one. Filing a return purely to preserve portability is
routine advice and it was not taken.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**The exclusion no longer sunsets.** It was scheduled to fall by roughly half after 2025 and does not.
Planning built around that expiry should be revisited.

**Portability requires an election on a filed return.** No return, no transferred exclusion, however
obvious the arithmetic.

**Gift basis is carryover; death basis is stepped.** This is the axis most current planning turns on.

**A retirement account gets no step-up** — § 1014(c) excludes income in respect of a decedent.

**Income tax and estate tax treat life insurance differently.** Excluded from income under § 101(a)(1);
included in the estate under § 2042 where there are incidents of ownership.

**The annual exclusion needs a present interest.** Gifts of future interests are outside § 2503(b),
which is why trust withdrawal rights exist.

**The unlimited marital deduction does not apply to a non-citizen spouse**; a larger annual exclusion
substitutes for it.

**Lifetime gifts consume the exclusion available at death.** The system is unified, so a gift is not free
merely because no gift tax was paid.
</div>

## How this has changed

Pub. L. 119-21 § 70106 amended § 2010(c)(3) by raising the basic exclusion amount and, as important,
resetting how it moves. The former text carried a base figure indexed from 2010 with a sunset that would
have cut the exclusion roughly in half after 2025. {fig:estate.basic_exclusion} Rev. Proc. 2025-32
§ 2.14 confirms the figure and confirms that the § 2631(c) generation-skipping exemption is the same
amount.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one that changes advice.

First, the cliff is gone. A great deal of planning done between 2018 and 2025 was driven by the prospect
of the exclusion halving — accelerated lifetime gifts, spousal lifetime access trusts, valuation
discounts pursued at speed. That pressure has been removed, and some of those structures are now
carrying costs for a benefit that no longer exists. They should be reviewed rather than assumed still to
be optimal.

Second, with the exclusion this high and permanent, the binding constraint for most families is income
tax rather than transfer tax. Section 1014 has become the more valuable provision, and the instinct to
move assets out of the estate is now frequently wrong for an estate below the exclusion. The right
question is no longer "how do we reduce the taxable estate" but "which assets should be held until death
so that their basis resets".

Nothing else on this page moved for 2026. The annual exclusion and the non-citizen spouse figure are the
ordinary inflation adjustments in Rev. Proc. 2025-32 § 4.42, and §§ 1014, 1015, 101 and 2042 are
unchanged.

## Exam focus

Know the basic exclusion amount, that it is unified across gift and estate tax, and that it no longer
sunsets. Know that the generation-skipping exemption matches it.

Know the annual exclusion, that it is per donee with no limit on donees, and that it requires a present
interest.

Know the § 1014 versus § 1015 contrast cold — stepped-up basis at death, carryover basis on a gift, with
the loss limitation using fair market value — because it is the most consequential distinction on the
topic and the one most likely to be tested.

Know that portability needs an election on a timely filed return, and that life insurance is outside
income tax but inside the gross estate where the decedent held incidents of ownership.

## Check yourself

**1.** A client gives appreciated shares to a child instead of leaving them by will. What is the income
tax consequence?

*Answer: The child takes the donor's basis under IRC § 1015(a), so the whole appreciation remains
taxable on a later sale. Had the shares passed at death, § 1014(a) would have given a basis equal to
fair market value at death and the appreciation would never have been taxed.*

**2.** An estate passes entirely to a surviving spouse and owes no estate tax. Is a return needed?

*Answer: Yes, if portability is wanted. IRC § 2010(c)(5)(A) makes the deceased spousal unused exclusion
amount available only where the first estate filed a timely and complete estate tax return electing it.
The tax owed is irrelevant to that requirement.*

**3.** Are life insurance proceeds taxable?

*Answer: Not for income tax — IRC § 101(a)(1) excludes amounts paid by reason of the insured's death.
But § 2042 includes them in the gross estate if receivable by the executor, or if the decedent possessed
any incident of ownership at death. The two systems reach opposite answers on the same money.*

**4.** How many donees can receive an annual exclusion gift?

*Answer: Any number. The IRC § 2503(b) exclusion applies per donee per calendar year with no limit on
the number of donees, provided each gift is of a present interest rather than a future interest.*

**5.** Does the basic exclusion amount fall back after 2025?

*Answer: No. Pub. L. 119-21 § 70106 amended IRC § 2010(c)(3) to set a new amount for calendar year
2026, indexed from calendar year 2025 for decedents dying after 2026. The scheduled reduction that drove
much 2018–2025 planning no longer applies.*
