<div class="plain-terms">
Tax planning at year-end is about timing. By November, most of a person's income for the year is
already fixed. What is not fixed is which year certain deductions, gifts, and payments count in, and
moving that date can change how much tax is owed. This page is for anyone deciding whether to pay a
bill, give to charity, or sell an investment at a loss before or after December 31. It affects nearly
every taxpayer who itemizes or invests, at least a little. It does not create new deductions. It only
decides which year an existing one lands in, and three long-standing rules stop the most obvious
shortcuts.
</div>

By the time a client sits down in November, the year's income is largely fixed. What is not fixed is
which year some of it falls in, and which year the deductions against it fall in. Timing is the last
lever, and it is a real one: moving a deduction across 31 December can change its value by the whole
difference between two marginal rates, or between deducting it and not deducting it at all. What makes
this hard is that the rules key off events — delivery, payment, availability — and not off the
taxpayer's intention or the accounting entry.

## The rule

**Income falls in the year received.** {fig:timing.cash_receipt} For almost every individual that is the
cash method, and the operative word is *received*.

**Constructive receipt reaches money the client has not touched.** {fig:timing.constructive_receipt} A
year-end bonus cheque available on 28 December is income in that year even if the client leaves it in a
drawer until January. The escape is the second sentence of the regulation, not the first: there must be
a substantial limitation or restriction on the taxpayer's control, imposed before the income is
available, not a decision to leave it uncollected.

**Deductions fall in the year paid.** {fig:timing.cash_payment} Payment by cheque is made on delivery or
mailing, provided the cheque clears in due course; payment by credit card is made when the card is
charged, because the card issuer has paid the payee. A promise to pay is not payment.

**Prepaid interest does not move.** {fig:timing.prepaid_interest} This is the rule that defeats the most
common year-end suggestion in practice. The exception is narrow: {fig:timing.points_exception}

**Contributions are made on delivery.** {fig:timing.charitable_delivery} The stock rule is the one to
watch, because the two routes give different dates for what looks like the same gift.

**Itemizing is an election.** {fig:timing.itemize_election} That is what makes bunching work: a client
who cannot clear the standard deduction in either of two years can often clear it in one of them by
compressing two years of deductible payments into a single year and taking the standard deduction in the
other.

**One deduction genuinely looks backwards.** {fig:timing.ira_deemed_made}

**Credits can be wasted by timing.** {fig:timing.nonrefundable_ceiling} A nonrefundable credit in a year
with little tax is worth little or nothing, and unlike a deduction it usually cannot be moved.

**Three rules block the obvious moves.** {fig:timing.wash_sale_window} {fig:timing.related_party_matching}
And the prepaid interest rule above.

## How it works in practice

Year-end planning for an individual is a search for a threshold the client is near but not over, because
that is where a moved dollar is worth more than its marginal rate.

**The standard deduction threshold.** A client whose itemizable deductions land just short of the
standard deduction gets nothing for them. Two years of charitable giving made in one year, or a January
property tax payment pulled into December, can put one year over the line while the other takes the
standard deduction. The whole strategy depends on § 63(e) being an annual election — it is, and it is
made on the return.

**The medical floor.** Medical expenses are deductible only above {fig:medical.floor}, which means a
client with scattered expenses in two years may deduct nothing in either, while the same expenses
concentrated in one year clear the floor. Elective procedures and dental work are the realistic
candidates; the payment, not the treatment, is what has to be moved.

**The state and local tax cap.** With the cap now at {fig:salt.cap} and phasing down at
{fig:salt.phasedown}, the calculus has changed from what it was under the flat limitation: a client
below the cap has room to accelerate a state estimated payment, and a client above it gets nothing for
doing so. Check where the client sits before suggesting it.

**The bracket-straddling year.** A client retiring mid-year, taking a sabbatical, or starting a business
has one low-income year next to a high one. Deferring a bonus into the low year, or accelerating a Roth
conversion into it, is worth the spread between the two marginal rates. The constructive receipt rule
sets the boundary: the deferral has to be agreed before the compensation is available, not after.

**The credit that will otherwise be lost.** A client with a large nonrefundable credit and a year of low
tax should think about *accelerating* income, not deferring it — there has to be a tax liability for the
credit to reduce (IRC § 26(a)). This is the one situation where the standard advice runs backwards, and
it is worth checking every time a credit carries forward on a return.

**Loss harvesting, done in the right order.** Selling a depreciated holding before year end produces a
capital loss that offsets realised gains. The wash sale rule sets the constraint, and the window runs in
both directions — a purchase 25 days *before* the sale disallows the loss just as a purchase 25 days
after does.

<div class="scenario" data-type="fails">
<h3>The mortgage payment that bought nothing</h3>

A client pays his January mortgage instalment on 28 December to get another month of interest into the
current year. The instalment includes $1,900 of interest accruing over January.

The deduction does not move. Under IRC § 461(g)(1) that interest is allocable to a period after the
close of the year, so it is charged to capital account and treated as paid in January. He has parted
with the money a week early and gained nothing. Had the payment instead covered December's interest and
merely been made late — in December rather than on the January due date — it would have been deductible,
because the interest would be allocable to December.

</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="timing">
<h3>Two routes for the same shares</h3>

A client wants to give appreciated stock worth $30,000 to her donor-advised fund and decides on
29 December. Her broker holds the shares in street name.

If she endorses a certificate and mails it to the charity on 29 December, the gift is made on the date
of mailing. If instead she instructs her broker to transfer the shares, Reg. § 1.170A-1(b) treats the
gift as made when the stock is transferred on the corporation's books — which, given the holidays, may
well be January. Same donor, same shares, same decision date, two different tax years. The instruction
has to go in early enough for the transfer to complete, and "I told my broker in December" is not the
test.

</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="baseline">
<h3>Bunching two years into one</h3>

A married couple has $9,000 of charitable giving and $6,000 of deductible state and local taxes a year,
against a {gloss:standard-deduction} they cannot clear. Itemizing gains them nothing in either year.

They make the 2026 and 2027 charitable gifts both in December 2026 — $18,000 — and pay the state
balance due in the same month. For 2026 they itemize; for 2027 they take the standard deduction and give
nothing. Nothing about their giving has changed except which side of 31 December the cheques fell on.
The mechanism is § 63(e): itemizing is elected year by year, so a year of large deductions and a year of
none is worth more than two average years.

</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="interaction">
<h3>The harvest that was undone in advance</h3>

A client sells a mutual fund position on 15 December at a $22,000 loss to offset a gain realised in
June. His account has a monthly automatic purchase of the same fund, which ran on 1 December.

The loss is disallowed to the extent of the shares bought on 1 December, because IRC § 1091(a) reaches
acquisitions within 30 days *before* the sale as well as after. Nobody chose to reacquire; a standing
instruction did it. The disallowed loss is not lost permanently — it is added to the basis of the
replacement shares — but it does not offset this year's gain, which was the entire point of the sale.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**"I'll just not cash the cheque."** {gloss:constructive-receipt} does not care. Under Reg. § 1.451-2(a) income
credited, set apart, or made available is received, and declining to collect it is not a substantial
limitation or restriction.

**Deferring compensation after it is earned and payable.** The agreement to defer has to be in place
before the amount is available. A November decision to take December's bonus in January is a decision
about money already made available.

**Treating a credit card charge as a promise to pay.** It is payment in the year charged. The client's
own debt to the card issuer is a separate matter and is not deductible.

**Prepaying interest.** IRC § 461(g)(1) reallocates it. The narrow points exception in § 461(g)(2)
applies to a principal residence purchase or improvement, not to a refinancing and not to a second home.

**Assuming an extension extends the IRA deadline.** IRC § 219(f)(3) uses the time prescribed for filing
*not including extensions*. An October return does not buy until October to fund the prior year's IRA.

**Accelerating a state tax payment without checking the cap.** Above {fig:salt.cap} the extra payment
buys no deduction at all, and the phase-down at {fig:salt.phasedown} can put a client back below the
line they thought they were above.

**Forgetting that the {gloss:wash-sale} window opens before the sale.** Automatic reinvestment of dividends and
standing purchase instructions are the usual culprits, and the client will not think of them as
purchases.

</div>

## How this has changed

The mechanics in this topic are among the oldest in the Code and have not moved. IRC § 451(a),
Reg. § 1.451-2, Reg. § 1.461-1(a)(1) and § 1091 are all substantially as they have stood for decades,
and Pub. L. 119-21 amended none of them.

What has changed is where the thresholds sit, and that changes the advice. The state and local tax cap
is now {fig:salt.cap} with {fig:salt.phasedown} — a very different planning environment from the flat
limitation that preceded it, {fig:salt.superseded_flat_cap}, under which most clients with any real
state tax exposure were above the cap and acceleration was pointless. Now a substantial number sit below it, and
the December state estimated payment is worth discussing again.

The medical floor has stopped moving: {fig:medical.floor} It spent a decade alternating between two
figures with repeated temporary extensions, and the current permanence means bunching medical expenses
can be planned over more than one year at a time.

The other material change is that the standard deduction is now large enough that a majority of clients
do not itemize at all in a normal year, which has moved bunching from a niche technique to the default
question to ask any client with meaningful charitable giving.

## Exam focus

Expect questions that give a date and ask for the year of deduction or inclusion. The reliable
distinctions are: cheque mailed versus cheque cashed (mailing, if it clears), credit card charged versus
card bill paid (charged), prepaid interest (reallocated), and stock delivered to the charity versus
handed to the taxpayer's own broker (delivery versus transfer on the books).

Constructive receipt is usually tested with a bonus available in December and collected in January —
income in December. The IRA look-back in § 219(f)(3) is tested with an extended return, where the answer
is that extensions do not count. The wash sale rule is tested with a purchase before the sale, because
candidates remember the 30 days after and forget the 30 days before.

Watch for questions where the right answer is to *accelerate* income: a nonrefundable credit with no tax
to absorb it, under § 26(a), is the standard setup.

## Check yourself

**1.** A client mails a cheque to a qualified charity on 31 December. The charity deposits it on
6 January and it clears on 9 January. In which year is the contribution deductible?

*Answer: The year of mailing. Under Reg. § 1.170A-1(b) the unconditional delivery or mailing of a cheque
that subsequently clears in due course is an effective contribution on the date of delivery or mailing.*

**2.** An employer tells an employee on 15 December that her bonus is available for collection any time
after 20 December. She asks the employer to hold it until January and it is paid on 4 January. When is
it income?

*Answer: In the earlier year. Reg. § 1.451-2(a) treats income as constructively received when it is set
apart or otherwise made available so that the taxpayer may draw on it. Her own request to defer
collection is not a substantial limitation or restriction on her control.*

**3.** A cash-method taxpayer pays her January mortgage instalment in December. What happens to the
interest portion?

*Answer: It is charged to capital account and treated as paid in January. IRC § 461(g)(1) applies to
interest allocable to a period after the close of the taxable year in which it is paid. The points
exception in § 461(g)(2) does not reach an ordinary instalment.*

**4.** A taxpayer files on extension in September and funds an IRA for the prior year at the same time.
Is the contribution deductible for the prior year?

*Answer: No. IRC § 219(f)(3) deems a contribution made on the last day of the preceding year only if it
is made by the time prescribed by law for filing the return for that year, not including extensions.*

**5.** A taxpayer sells stock at a loss on 10 December, having bought additional shares of the same
stock on 20 November. Is the loss allowed?

*Answer: No, to the extent of the replacement shares. IRC § 1091(a) disallows a loss where substantially
identical stock is acquired within the period beginning 30 days before the sale and ending 30 days
after it. The 20 November purchase falls inside the window, and the disallowed loss is added to the
basis of those shares.*
