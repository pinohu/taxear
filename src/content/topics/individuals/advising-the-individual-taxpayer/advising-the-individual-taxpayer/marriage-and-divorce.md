<div class="plain-terms">
Getting married or divorced changes several tax rules at once. The changes rarely land where people
expect. Your marital status for the whole year is fixed by one single date. Whether alimony is taxed at
all depends only on when the divorce paper was signed. And splitting property evenly in a divorce does
not always split the tax bill evenly too. This topic affects anyone who married, divorced, or split up
during the year. It also touches how a child is claimed afterward. It decides which filing status
applies, whether alimony counts as income, and what tax bill each spouse is left with once the assets are
split.
</div>

Three things about a divorce carry most of the tax consequence, and none of them is the decree itself.
Marital status is tested on a single day. Alimony stopped being a tax item for new instruments in 2019.
And a settlement that divides assets evenly by value usually divides them unevenly after tax, because
§ 1041 hands the recipient the transferor's basis along with the property.

## The rule

**Status is a one-day test** (IRC § 7703(a)). {fig:divorce.marital_status} A couple married for eleven
months and divorced on 31 December is unmarried for the whole year; a couple who married on 31 December is married
for the whole year. Length of time is irrelevant.

**Alimony.** {fig:divorce.alimony_repeal} This is the change most likely to be misremembered, because it
operates by execution date rather than by tax year: a 2015 instrument still produces a deduction and an
inclusion in 2026, while a 2019 instrument never did and never will.

**Modifying an old instrument.** A pre-2019 instrument keeps the old treatment unless it is modified
after 2018 *and* the modification expressly provides that the repeal applies. So a modification is a
decision point, and an adviser should know which side of it the client wants to be on before the
document is signed.

**Property transfers** (IRC § 1041). {fig:divorce.property_transfer} And {fig:divorce.incident_test}.
The non-recognition is not a permanent exemption — it is a deferral, and the gain lands on whoever ends up
selling.

**The base amount for social security** (IRC § 86(c)(1)). {fig:divorce.social_security_base} The zero
case is the trap: a
separated couple who file separately and lived together at any point during the year have a base amount
of nothing, so their benefits are taxable from the first dollar of other income.

**Children** (IRC § 152(e)). {fig:divorce.dependency} The release is on Form 8332 and it moves the child tax credit and
the education credits — it does *not* move head of household status, the earned income credit or the
dependent care credit, each of which stays with the custodial parent. That split is covered at 1.3.2.a
and 1.3.2.e.

**Joint liability survives the divorce.** A joint return makes the tax joint and several under
§ 6013(d)(3), and a decree apportioning the liability binds the spouses, not the Service. Relief has to
come from § 6015 — covered at 1.5.1.i — and a refund taken for one spouse's separate debt is dealt with
at 1.5.1.h.

**Community property.** In a community property state, income earned during the marriage is generally
split between the spouses regardless of who earned it, and separation does not automatically end that.
The consequences reach filing status, withholding credits and injured spouse allocations alike.

## How it works in practice

**Fix the date first.** Everything on the return follows from marital status at 31 December, and clients
routinely assume the tax year splits with the marriage. It does not.

**Then price the settlement after tax, not before.** A house and a brokerage account of the same value
are not equal if their bases differ. Section 1041(b)(2) gives the recipient the transferor's basis, so
the spouse taking the low-basis asset carries an unrealised gain the other does not. Raise it while the
settlement is still negotiable.

**Then check the alimony instrument's date, not its terms.** Payments under a pre-2019 order remain
deductible and includible; nothing about the label matters if the instrument is newer.

**Then work the filing statuses both ways.** For a couple still married at year end, compare joint
against separate — including the § 86(c)(1)(C) base amount, the loss of several credits on a separate
return, and the joint and several liability that a joint return creates.

**And deal with the children explicitly.** Who claims the child, and for which benefit, should be
written into the agreement, with the Form 8332 release addressed rather than assumed.

<div class="scenario" data-type="interaction">
<h3>The equal split that was not</h3>

Hafsa and Deji divide two assets of equal value: the family home, worth $500,000 with a basis of
$120,000, and an investment account worth $500,000 with a basis of $470,000. Hafsa takes the house.

Section 1041(a) means neither recognises gain on the division, and § 1041(b)(2) gives each the
transferor's {gloss:basis}. Hafsa is carrying $380,000 of unrealised gain and Deji $30,000. If Hafsa later sells
and qualifies for the § 121 exclusion, much of hers may still escape; if she does not, or if the gain
exceeds the exclusion, she bears a tax Deji does not. On these facts the "equal" split favoured Deji by
a sum worth negotiating over.
</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="boundary">
<h3>Separated, filing separately, and taxed from the first dollar</h3>

A man aged 68 files for divorce during the year and moves out in October. He and his wife lived together
until then. He files separately for the year.

He is still married at the close of the year under § 7703(a)(1), because the divorce is not final. He
files separately, and he did not live apart from his spouse *at all times* during the year. Section
86(c)(1)(C) therefore gives him a base amount of zero, so his social security benefits are taxable from
the first dollar of other income. Had the divorce been final by 31 December, or had they lived apart for
the whole year, the base amount would have been $25,000.
</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="procedural">
<h3>The modification that changed the deal</h3>

A 2016 decree requires monthly alimony. In 2026 the parties agree to reduce the amount and sign a
modification. The drafter, working from a form, includes a clause stating that the amendments made by
section 11051 of Public Law 115-97 apply.

That single clause moves the payments out of the old regime: the payer loses the deduction and the
recipient stops including the payments. If the reduction was negotiated on the assumption that the payer
would keep the deduction, the economics of the agreement have changed and neither party may realise it.
Without the clause, the pre-2019 treatment would have continued despite the modification.
</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="timing">
<h3>One day either side of the line</h3>

Two couples come in for a consultation on the same afternoon. The first married on 2 January of the year
and has been married ever since. The second married on 30 December of the same year, after dating for six
weeks.

Both couples are married for the entire year for federal tax purposes. IRC § 7703(a) tests marital status
as of the last day of the taxable year, not the number of days actually married during it — the couple
married for 363 days and the couple married for 2 days file exactly the same filing statuses. A divorce
finalised on 31 December instead of 2 January the following year would flip either couple to unmarried for
the whole year just as sharply.
</div>

<div class="scenario" data-type="baseline">
<h3>The release that moves the credit, not the parent</h3>

Divorced parents share custody, and their daughter lives with her mother for 220 nights a year. The mother
signs Form 8332 releasing the dependency-linked benefits to the father for the year.

The father can claim the {gloss:child-tax-credit} for their {gloss:qualifying-child} using the release,
but nothing else moves. The mother keeps head of household status, the earned income credit, and the
dependent care credit, because those benefits are tied to which parent the child actually lived with, not
to who holds the release. A parent who assumes the release hands over "the dependent" in every sense is in
for a surprise at filing time.
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**Status is tested at year end**, or at death. Eleven months of marriage counts for nothing if the
decree lands on 30 December.

**Alimony turns on the execution date of the instrument**, not the payment date and not the tax year.

**A modification only changes the regime if it says so.** The election is express, not automatic.

**Section 1041 defers gain; it does not forgive it.** The recipient takes the transferor's basis.

**"Incident to the divorce" has two limbs** — within one year of the marriage ceasing, or related to
the cessation.

**The social security base amount can be zero.** Married, filing separately, and living together at any
point in the year is the combination.

**Form 8332 does not move everything.** The dependency-linked credits move; head of household, the
earned income credit and the dependent care credit stay with the custodial parent.

**A decree does not bind the Service.** Joint and several liability under § 6013(d)(3) survives it.
</div>

## How this has changed

Nothing in Pub. L. 119-21 touched this topic. The change that still dominates it is Pub. L. 115-97
§ 11051, which repealed §§ 71 and 215 outright for any divorce or separation instrument executed after
31 December 2018.

Two features of that repeal make it unusually long-lived as a source of error. It is keyed to the
execution date of the instrument rather than to a tax year, so both regimes are live simultaneously and
will be for decades — a 2018 decree governs payments made in 2040. And the transition rule for
modifications requires an express adoption of the repeal, which means the same 2016 decree can produce
different answers before and after an amendment depending on a clause that has nothing to do with the
amount being changed.

The practical consequence is that the first question about any alimony payment is the date on the
document, and the second is whether it has ever been modified. Neither is answered by the client's
description of the payments, and both should be checked against the instrument itself.

Section 682, which had allocated trust income to a beneficiary former spouse, was repealed by the same
provision and on the same terms, which is worth knowing where a settlement used a trust rather than
direct payments.

## Exam focus

Know that marital status is determined at the close of the taxable year, or at death, and that the
length of the marriage during the year is irrelevant.

Know that alimony under an instrument executed after 2018 is neither deductible nor includible, that
pre-2019 instruments keep the old treatment, and that a modification changes the regime only if it says
so expressly.

Know § 1041: no gain or loss, carryover basis, and the two limbs of "incident to the divorce".

Know the three base amounts under § 86(c)(1) and in particular the zero case, because it is the one that
produces a surprising answer.

And know which benefits a Form 8332 release moves and which it does not.

## Check yourself

**1.** A couple divorce on 5 December. What filing status is available for that year?

*Answer: Single, or head of household if its separate conditions are met. IRC § 7703(a)(1) determines
marital status as of the close of the taxable year, so a decree entered in December makes both parties
unmarried for the whole year regardless of how long they were married during it.*

**2.** A 2015 divorce decree requires alimony. Is a payment made in 2026 deductible?

*Answer: Yes. Pub. L. 115-97 § 11051 repealed IRC §§ 71 and 215 only for instruments executed after
31 December 2018. A pre-2019 instrument keeps the deduction and the corresponding inclusion, unless it
has been modified after 2018 with an express provision that the repeal applies.*

**3.** One spouse takes a house with a low basis and the other an account of equal value with a high
basis. Is the division tax-neutral?

*Answer: Not economically. IRC § 1041(a) means neither recognises gain on the transfer, but § 1041(b)(2)
gives each the transferor's adjusted basis, so the spouse taking the low-basis asset carries the larger
unrealised gain. Equal value is not equal after tax.*

**4.** A married taxpayer files separately and lived with their spouse for part of the year. What is the
base amount for taxing social security benefits?

*Answer: Zero. IRC § 86(c)(1)(C) sets the base amount at zero for a taxpayer who is married at the close
of the year, does not file a joint return, and does not live apart from their spouse at all times during
the year. Benefits are taxable from the first dollar of other income.*

**5.** Does a divorce decree assigning the tax liability to one spouse protect the other from the IRS?

*Answer: No. A joint return creates joint and several liability under IRC § 6013(d)(3), and a decree
binds the spouses rather than the Service. The only relief runs through § 6015, on its own conditions
and deadlines.*

**6. A 2016 divorce decree requires monthly alimony. In 2026 the parties sign a modification reducing the amount, and the modification says nothing about the alimony rules. How are payments made after the modification treated?**
(A) Neither deductible nor includible, because any post-2018 modification brings the instrument under the current rules (B) Still deductible by the payer and includible by the recipient, because the modification did not expressly adopt the repeal (C) Deductible by the payer but no longer includible by the recipient (D) Includible by the recipient but no longer deductible by the payer
*Answer: B. Pub. L. 115-97 § 11051: a pre-2019 instrument keeps the old treatment unless it is modified after 2018 and the modification expressly provides that the repeal applies. The election is express, not automatic, so a silent modification leaves the old regime in place.*

**7. Divorced parents share custody, and their daughter lives with her mother for 220 nights a year. The mother signs Form 8332 releasing the claim to the father for the year. Which benefit moves to the father?**
(A) Head of household filing status (B) The earned income credit (C) The child tax credit (D) The child and dependent care credit
*Answer: C. IRC § 152(e): the custodial parent's release lets the noncustodial parent claim the dependency-linked benefits, the child tax credit and the education credits, but head of household status, the earned income credit and the dependent care credit stay with the parent the child actually lived with.*

**8. A divorce becomes final on 1 March 2026. Under the property settlement, one former spouse transfers a rental property to the other on 15 September 2026. Does § 1041 apply to the transfer?**
(A) No, because § 1041 covers only transfers made while the parties are still married (B) Yes, because a transfer within one year after the marriage ceases is incident to the divorce (C) Only if the transfer is also shown to be related to the cessation of the marriage (D) No, so the recipient recognises gain equal to the property's appreciation
*Answer: B. IRC § 1041(c)(1), (2): a transfer is incident to the divorce if it occurs within one year after the marriage ceases or is related to the cessation of the marriage. The two limbs are alternatives, so the September transfer qualifies on timing alone; no gain or loss is recognised and the recipient takes the transferor's basis under § 1041(b)(2).*
