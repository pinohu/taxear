An examination is a demand for proof of what the return says. The obligation to be able to supply that proof is not created by the examination — it exists from the moment the return is filed, and it comes from IRC § 6001. What changes at examination is only that someone is asking. The topic has two levels: a general standard that applies to every entry, and a **stricter** standard that applies to three categories where the general one has been displaced.

## The rule

**The general obligation.** "Every person liable for any tax imposed by this title, or for the collection thereof, shall **keep such records, render such statements, make such returns**, and comply with such rules and regulations as the Secretary may from time to time prescribe" (IRC § 6001). The Secretary may also require records of a particular person "by notice served upon such person or by regulations."

**What "such records" means.** The regulation supplies the standard — see the figures table (Reg. § 1.6001-1(a)). Two carve-outs sit beside it. **Farmers and wage-earners** need only keep records that will enable the Service "to determine the correct amount of income subject to the tax," and need **not** keep the permanent books of account required generally (Reg. § 1.6001-1(b)). **Exempt organisations** must, in addition, keep records sufficient to show specifically the items of gross income, receipts and disbursements, and to substantiate the Form 990 information (Reg. § 1.6001-1(c)).

**Retention.** See the figures table (Reg. § 1.6001-1(e)). Note what that standard is **not**: it is not a fixed number of years. It runs to materiality, which is why records supporting basis in an asset held for thirty years must be kept for thirty years and beyond.

**The examination power that reaches them.** For the purpose of ascertaining the correctness of any return the Secretary is authorised "to examine any books, papers, records, or other data which may be **relevant or material** to such inquiry" (IRC § 7602(a)(1)).

**The stricter standard — IRC § 274(d).** For three categories of expense, listed in the figures table, "**no deduction or credit shall be allowed** … **unless** the taxpayer substantiates **by adequate records or by sufficient evidence corroborating the taxpayer's own statement**" each of four elements — also in the figures table (IRC § 274(d)).

Read the operative words carefully. The deduction is disallowed **unless** substantiated. That is a **condition of the deduction**, not a rule about who carries a burden. A § 274(d) item that is not substantiated is disallowed even where the examiner accepts that the expense was incurred and was ordinary and necessary.

**Information returns.** Where a taxpayer asserts "a **reasonable dispute**" with an item of income on a third-party information return and "has **fully cooperated**" with the Secretary — including giving timely access to witnesses, information and documents within their control — the Secretary "shall have the **burden of producing reasonable and probative information** concerning such deficiency **in addition to** such information return" (IRC § 6201(d)). By its terms this applies "**in any court proceeding**."

## How it works in practice

**The obligation is the taxpayer's, and it is continuous.** Section 6001 does not say "when examined" — it says every person liable for tax shall keep records. A client who discarded records once the return was accepted has not described a defence; they have described a failure to comply with § 6001 that will be resolved against them on the items they cannot prove.

**"Sufficient to establish" is the whole test for ordinary items.** For anything outside § 274(d), the question is whether the records establish the amount. Cancelled cheques, invoices, logs, bank records and third-party statements all count; no particular document is required. Where records are incomplete but the expense plainly was incurred, an examiner has room to allow a reasonable estimate, and often will.

**That room does not exist under § 274(d).** This is the single most important operational distinction in the topic. For travel, gifts and listed property, an approximation is not a substitute for substantiation, because the statute conditions the deduction on substantiating each element. An examiner who would accept a reasonable estimate of office supplies cannot accept one of vehicle mileage.

**Learn the four elements as a checklist, because that is how they are examined.** Amount; time and place (or, for a gift, the date and description); business purpose; and business relationship to the taxpayer of the person receiving the benefit. A mileage log giving date and distance but not purpose fails on the third element. A meal receipt showing amount, date and restaurant but not who was present fails on the fourth. Partial substantiation of a § 274(d) item is not partial allowance — the elements are conjunctive.

**"Adequate records **or** sufficient evidence corroborating the taxpayer's own statement" is a real alternative.** The statute offers two routes. The second is not "the taxpayer's word": it is the taxpayer's statement **plus corroboration**. A reconstructed log supported by calendar entries, client billing records and third-party confirmations can satisfy § 274(d). A reconstructed log supported by nothing cannot.

**Section 6201(d) is narrower than practitioners hope.** It applies **in a court proceeding**, and it requires **both** a reasonable dispute **and** full cooperation, including timely access to witnesses, information and documents. Its effect is to stop the Service resting on the information return alone — it makes the Service produce reasonable and probative information in addition. It does not shift the burden of proof, and it is not an examination-stage rule the representative can invoke against a revenue agent, though raising it early and documenting cooperation is exactly how a taxpayer preserves it.

**Cooperation is a substantive step, not a courtesy.** Section 6201(d) conditions its protection on full cooperation, and § 274(d)'s corroboration route depends on assembling third-party material. A representative who lets a document request sit is spending the client's substantive position.

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The mileage log without a purpose</h3>
<p>A sole proprietor produces a spreadsheet showing, for each business trip in 2025, the date, the odometer readings and the total miles. The examiner disallows the entire vehicle deduction. The client protests that the log is meticulous.</p>
<p><em>Analysis.</em> It is meticulous and it is incomplete. A passenger vehicle is listed property, so IRC § 274(d) applies, and the deduction is allowed only if the taxpayer substantiates the amount, the time and place, <em>the business purpose</em>, and the business relationship of the person receiving the benefit. The log establishes amount and time and place; it says nothing about purpose. The route to a deduction is corroboration — calendar entries, client records, invoices tied to the dates — not an argument that the mileage figures are reliable.</p>
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The estimate that was accepted, and the one that was not</h3>
<p>An examination questions two items: $6,200 of shop supplies, for which the client has bank statements but no invoices, and $4,100 of client travel, for which the client has credit card statements showing airline and hotel charges but no record of who was seen or why.</p>
<p><em>Analysis.</em> Different rules. The supplies are an ordinary business expense outside § 274(d); the test is whether the records are sufficient to establish the amount, and bank statements showing payments to identifiable suppliers may carry it, with the examiner free to allow a reasonable figure. The travel is squarely within § 274(d), and credit card statements establish amount and time and place but neither business purpose nor business relationship. Without corroboration of those two elements, the travel is disallowed in full — not reduced.</p>
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The 1099 that was wrong</h3>
<p>A client receives a Form 1099-NEC reporting $48,000 from a customer who in fact paid $28,000; the payer double-counted a reversed transaction and will not issue a correction. The client gives the representative bank records, invoices and the customer's own remittance advices, and the representative supplies all of it to the examiner within the deadline. The examiner proposes a deficiency on the full $48,000.</p>
<p><em>Analysis.</em> This is what IRC § 6201(d) is for, though not yet. Where the taxpayer asserts a reasonable dispute with an item of income on a third-party information return and has fully cooperated — timely access to witnesses, information and documents within their control — the Secretary must produce reasonable and probative information <em>in addition to</em> the information return. The provision operates "in any court proceeding," so it is not a rule the representative can enforce against the examiner. What the representative is doing now is building the record of reasonable dispute and full cooperation that makes it available later.</p>
</div>

<div class="scenario">
<h3>The records that were thrown away</h3>
<p>An examination of 2023 questions the basis claimed on the sale of a rental property bought in 1998. The client kept returns but discarded the closing documents and improvement invoices years ago, saying the statute of limitations had long run on the purchase year.</p>
<p><em>Analysis.</em> The limitations period on the 1998 return is irrelevant. Reg. § 1.6001-1(e) requires records to be retained "so long as the contents thereof may become material in the administration of any internal revenue law" — and basis records remain material until the asset is disposed of and that year closes. The obligation ran from 1998 to 2023 and beyond. The representative's work is reconstruction from county records, lender files and contractor records, not an argument that the retention period expired.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**Retention is not a fixed number of years.** It runs "so long as the contents may become **material**" — which for basis records means the life of the asset plus the open period on the year of disposition.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**Section 274(d) is a condition of the deduction, not a burden rule.** An unsubstantiated travel, gift or listed-property item is disallowed even where the examiner believes the expense was incurred.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**The four elements are conjunctive.** Missing one — usually business purpose or business relationship — disallows the item entirely. Partial substantiation is not partial allowance.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">

**IRC § 6201(d) applies in a court proceeding**, and only where the taxpayer both asserts a **reasonable dispute** and has **fully cooperated**. It makes the Service produce more than the information return; it does not shift the burden of proof.

</div>

## How this has changed

**Entertainment left § 274(d), and the deduction left with it.** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 repealed the deduction for entertainment, amusement and recreation expenses for amounts paid or incurred after 31 December 2017. The current § 274(d) list is travel, gifts and listed property — entertainment is no longer in it, because there is generally no longer an entertainment deduction to substantiate. Material listing "entertainment" as a fourth § 274(d) category is pre-2018.

**Listed property lost the computer.** TCJA § 13202(b)(1) struck the clause covering "any computer or peripheral equipment" from IRC § 280F(d)(4)(A), for property placed in service after 31 December 2017. What remains listed property is passenger automobiles, other property used as a means of transportation, property of a type generally used for entertainment, recreation or amusement, and property specified by regulation — so entertainment-type *property* is still listed property even though entertainment *expenses* are no longer deductible. Vehicles are where almost all § 274(d) examination work is.

**Section 6201(d) is a 1996 addition.** It came in with the second Taxpayer Bill of Rights, and it responded to a practice of proposing deficiencies on information returns alone. It is worth knowing as the answer to "what protects a taxpayer against a wrong Form 1099," while knowing precisely what it does and does not do.

**The regulation's vocabulary is dated.** Reg. § 1.6001-1(b) and (d) still refer to the "**district director**," an office the IRS restructured out of existence after the IRS Restructuring and Reform Act of 1998. The standards are current; the title is not.

## Exam focus

Know that **IRC § 6001** creates a **continuing** obligation to keep records, and that the standard is records **sufficient to establish** the amounts shown on the return (Reg. § 1.6001-1(a)).

Know that retention runs to **materiality**, not to a fixed number of years.

Know the **three § 274(d) categories** — travel, gifts, listed property — and that **entertainment is no longer among them**.

Know the **four § 274(d) elements** and that they are **conjunctive**: amount; time and place, or date and description of a gift; business purpose; business relationship of the recipient.

Know the two § 274(d) routes: **adequate records**, or **sufficient evidence corroborating the taxpayer's own statement** — the second being the taxpayer's statement **plus** corroboration, not the statement alone.

Know that **IRC § 6201(d)** applies in a **court proceeding**, requires a **reasonable dispute** and **full cooperation**, and makes the Service produce reasonable and probative information **in addition to** the information return.

## Check yourself

**1. How long must records supporting the basis of a rental property be kept?**
(A) Three years from the return's due date (B) Six years (C) So long as their contents may become material in the administration of any internal revenue law (D) Until the property is placed in service
*Answer: C. Reg. § 1.6001-1(e). Basis records stay material until the year of disposition closes.*

**2. Which is no longer one of the categories subject to the IRC § 274(d) substantiation rules?**
(A) Travel expenses (B) Gifts (C) Entertainment (D) Listed property
*Answer: C. TCJA repealed the entertainment deduction for amounts paid or incurred after 31 December 2017, and the current § 274(d) list is travel, gifts and listed property.*

**3. A taxpayer substantiates the amount, date and location of a business meal but cannot show who attended or why. What is the result?**
(A) A reasonable portion is allowed (B) The item is disallowed — the elements are conjunctive (C) The examiner must accept the taxpayer's statement (D) The item is allowed if the amount is small
*Answer: B. Business purpose and business relationship are separate elements, and § 274(d) allows no deduction unless each is substantiated.*

**4. What does "sufficient evidence corroborating the taxpayer's own statement" permit?**
(A) The taxpayer's testimony alone (B) An estimate, if reasonable (C) The taxpayer's statement supported by corroborating evidence (D) Nothing — only adequate records will do
*Answer: C. It is a genuine alternative to adequate records, but it requires corroboration; the statement standing alone is not enough.*

**5. In what setting does IRC § 6201(d) place a production requirement on the Secretary?**
(A) At the examination, on request (B) In Appeals (C) In any court proceeding, where the taxpayer asserts a reasonable dispute and has fully cooperated (D) At any stage, without condition
*Answer: C. Both conditions must be met, and the provision operates by its terms "in any court proceeding."*
